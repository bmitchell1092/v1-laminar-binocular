# Thalamocortical mechanisms behind the order of CSD sink and spiking onset at the input layer (cat, rodent, rabbit, models)

Scope: non-primate laminar work (cat, rat, mouse, gerbil, plus rabbit, which belongs to neither group but is the main source of single-axon thalamocortical CSD data) and foundational CSD/LFP theory. Primate V1 laminar papers are excluded. "Verified" means the statement was read in the full text or abstract during this session. "[snippet]" means it comes only from a search-engine summary and was not read in the paper. "[unverified]" means it is from prior knowledge and was not checked this session.

## Q1. Mitzdorf (1985) and Mitzdorf & Singer: presynaptic fiber volleys versus postsynaptic sinks, and their timing in cat visual cortex

### Takeaway
Mitzdorf (1985) concluded that CSDs are "essentially" caused by EPSPs. Action potentials in fibers of passage are spread over 1–2 mm and cannot be resolved by laminar CSD. Somatic action potentials are negligible during natural (dissipated) activation. Activation of terminal arborizations does produce a localized sink/source pair, and Mitzdorf cites the primary afferent activity of the neocortex and LGN as examples, but she left terminal activity out of her quantitative estimate because "the details of this process are not known well enough." In cat areas 17 and 18, the earliest sinks (layer IV, plus layer VI) were identified as monosynaptic EPSP currents from geniculate afferents. The parts of the review I extracted give no millisecond latencies separating a presynaptic component from the first sink.

### Cited Findings
- **Citation.** Mitzdorf U (1985). "Current source-density method and application in cat cerebral cortex: investigation of evoked potentials and EEG phenomena." *Physiol Rev* 65(1):37–100. DOI 10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37. Quotes below are from the OCR text layer of the scanned PDF, with OCR errors corrected. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37); [PDF](https://docs.backyardbrains.com/assets/files/Mitzdorf-1985-62c16807b94586a7ef1ee976c48b39ec.pdf)
- **Fiber APs at the membrane.** "Activations of axons cause triphasic membrane currents, inwardly directed at the central site of activation and outwardly directed in front of and behind this zone… the spatial extent of this triphasic membrane current along the axon is proportional to the conduction velocity of the fiber. A conduction velocity of 20 m/s, for example, implies that the instantaneous membrane current is distributed over 20 mm!" (p. 44). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Terminal invasion (historical view).** "Generally it is assumed that the active spike does not invade the presynaptic terminals in the central nervous system (CNS); they are depolarized by the capacitive outward current that flows in front of the AP" (p. 44). This was the working assumption in 1985. Later single-axon work in rabbit and cat describes a terminal "spike" (see Q2). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Fibers of passage cannot be resolved.** "Activation of fibers results in a source/sink/source distribution… Even for the slowest types of fibers, it extends over 1–2 mm. This implies that the amplitudes of the sink and the two sources are low… Furthermore, this dissipated CSD distribution is not resolvable in the usual type of CSD applications designed to study a cortex or a nucleus and optimized to resolve events that extend over ~50–300 µm" (p. 49). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Terminal arborizations do produce a local CSD.** "In contrast to the activation of fibers of passage, the activation of terminal arborizations results in a localized sink/source distribution (196, 197). The sink probably results from the last active inward current, and the source in front of it is due to the leading capacitive outward current… this interpretation must remain tentative." And: "Temporal dissipation of the afferent impulses leads to a prolongation and a proportional amplitude reduction of the CSD. This temporal scatter does not cause any cancellation, because the time course of the CSD is monophasic. Examples of this type of activation are the primary afferent activities of the neocortex (197) and of the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) (196)" (p. 49). Ref 196 is Mitzdorf & Singer 1977 (cat LGN). Ref 197 falls between 196 and ref 198 (Mitzdorf & Singer, "Excitatory synaptic…") in the reference list, so it is presumably Mitzdorf & Singer 1978, Exp Brain Res (inferred from the list order). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Terminals excluded from the quantitative estimate.** "Activations of fiber terminals were not included in this estimate because the details of this process are not known well enough to make the appropriate assumptions" (p. 51, Table 1 text). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Relative sizes of the generators.** "According to this theoretical estimate, EPSPs cause the largest CSD amplitudes… the EPSPs still cause two to three times larger CSDs than APs in fibers or cell somata. The APs, although having much higher amplitude, are less effective, either because they are very dissipated (fiber APs) or because the fraction of the loss current from the soma is very small (soma APs). During natural activation, soma APs are totally negligible in comparison with EPSPs… The IPSPs are an order of magnitude less effective" (p. 51). Also: "Even during coherent activations, however, AP CSDs are still about five times smaller than the EPSP CSDs. Only if the scatter of the APs is very low may their contribution become about equally significant" (p. 54). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Why spike scatter cancels but synaptic scatter does not.** Somatic AP CSDs are biphasic in time, so "temporal scatter of the APs also causes cancellations." Cancellation "steadily increases up to a scatter of ~1.5 ms," after which "all the membrane currents from the successive APs cancel totally; only the first phase of the earliest APs and the last phase of the very last APs remain imbalanced" (p. 50). Synaptic and terminal CSDs are monophasic, so temporal scatter only prolongs them and reduces their amplitude (p. 49–50). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **CSD reflects the rate of change of the EPSP.** The capacitive current "is proportional to the time derivative, i.e., to the slope of the local PSP… The return of the intracellular potential from the PSP peak to equilibrium essentially results from the discharging of the membrane capacitors via the local membrane resistors; therefore, no net membrane current flows during this decay phase" (p. 45). Within 0.5–1 length constant of the synapse, the passive return current is "reduced to 10–30%, its time function is dissipated by a factor of ~2, and the amplitude peak is delayed by ~0.5–2 ms" (p. 45). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Upper-bound estimate of AP contribution, cat area 18.** Area 18 monosynaptic activation is where "APs contribute most, because the latency scatter is very low (0.4 ms)." With double-shock stimulation, "the sink of the second response is reduced by 30%… and the number of corresponding APs is reduced by 56%… which implies a contribution of 54% from the APs." Mitzdorf adds that "this estimate appears to be too high" (p. 54). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Pharmacological test.** After systemic picrotoxin and pentobarbital, "The small drug effects on the monosynaptic components (3–10%) further indicate that even the monosynaptic components mainly result from synaptic activity and only to a small extent from APs" (p. 60). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Unit–field correlations do not show that spikes generate the field.** "The correlations found in many studies, between single-unit responses and peaks of the evoked potentials… do not contradict the assumption that EPSPs, not APs, are the main causes of the evoked potentials. Because EPSPs cause the APs, such a correlation is to be expected" (p. 54). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Antidromic APs.** Antidromic APs in deep pyramidal cells after optic-radiation stimulation have "a latency scatter of ~6 ms… no contribution of these scattered APs to CSDs was found (197)" (pp. 54–55). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Separating pre- from postsynaptic components.** "Train stimuli or double stimuli may help to distinguish between pre- and postsynaptic components, or between mono- and polysynaptic components, according to the differences in their recovery cycles" (p. 55). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Cat areas 17/18, electrical stimulation.** Optic radiation, chiasm and nerve were stimulated, with recordings in pentobarbital and/or N2O anesthesia. "The earliest sinks and sources of each group could then be attributed to monosynaptic activity and all later components to polysynaptic activity." Fig. 3 legend: "Sinks a, b, and c reflect mono-, di-, and trisynaptic Y-type activity; sinks d and f reflect mono-, di-, and trisynaptic X-type activity; sink e reflects Y-type and X-type monosynaptic activity." "The monosynaptic sinks (a, d, and e…) demonstrate that the afferent fibers terminate in laminae IV and VI. In lamina IV the Y-type afferents terminate above the X-type afferents" (pp. 58–61). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Monosynaptic sinks survive cortical inhibition.** At the second of a pair of optic-radiation shocks, inhibition "does not affect the monosynaptic activation, but it prevents the generation of APs in many cells. Accordingly, the sinks and sources, which had been attributed to monosynaptic activation, were still present… those components that had been attributed to polysynaptic activation, however, were drastically reduced or totally missing" (p. 59). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Visually evoked CSDs.** These were "very similar" to electrically evoked ones: "early sinks are apparent in input layers IV and VI (sinks a, d, and e) in all CSDs. A sink in layer III (b) follows, slightly delayed." Brighter steps produce "a CSD that has a shorter latency, is compressed in time, and has higher amplitudes… attributed to the shorter latency and the higher coherence of the afferent input." The relative onsets of the layer IV and layer VI sinks depend on contour content (pp. 63–67). — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Mitzdorf & Singer 1978.** "Prominent excitatory pathways in the cat visual cortex (A 17 and A 18): a current source density analysis of electrically evoked potentials." *Exp Brain Res* 33:371–394 (PMID 215431). Optic nerves were stimulated electrically and the responses analyzed with one-dimensional CSD. The abstract and full text were not retrieved. — [DOI 10.1007/BF00235560](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF00235560)
- **Mitzdorf & Singer 1977.** "Laminar segregation of afferents to lateral geniculate nucleus of the cat: an analysis of current source density." *J Neurophysiol* 40(6):1227–1244. Bibliographic details only. — [index record](https://itts023d.itts.ttu.edu/OrDB/Data/6128)

### Inferences
- In Mitzdorf's framework, a thalamocortical terminal volley can add a small, localized, monophasic sink/source pair to the layer IV CSD. Fibers of passage add nothing resolvable. The sink measured in layer IV is dominated by monosynaptic EPSP current, and that current necessarily follows the arrival of the presynaptic impulse.
- Mitzdorf's cancellation argument cuts both ways for an MUA-versus-CSD comparison. Spikes, including those of postsynaptic L4 cells, are largely invisible in the CSD under natural stimulation, so the CSD sink cannot be "made of" the spikes seen in MUA. The CSD sink time course is set by summed synaptic current, which keeps growing through polysynaptic recruitment. The layer III sink b "follows, slightly delayed" the monosynaptic layer IV sink.

### Gaps
- No millisecond latencies were extracted that separate a presynaptic terminal component from the first postsynaptic layer IV sink in cat after optic-radiation, chiasm or visual stimulation. These values are probably in Mitzdorf & Singer 1978 (Exp Brain Res), which could not be retrieved. Pages 52–53 of the 1985 OCR text (sections IIC3c–e) were also not recovered.
- Mitzdorf (1985) does not compare CSD sink onset with multiunit onset in layer IV. Unit data are used only to support the argument from temporal coincidence.

## Q2. Thalamocortical axon and terminal activity in L4 MUA and LFP: how much of L4 MUA is thalamic, and how early

### Takeaway
Single-axon spike-triggered CSD in awake rabbit S1 and V1 and in cat V1 consistently shows a brief biphasic/triphasic presynaptic "axon-terminal potential" (aTP) in layer 4. It occurs about 0.8 ms after the thalamic spike (cat: 0.8–2.1 ms), and the postsynaptic monosynaptic sink starts about 0.5–0.7 ms after the aTP, about 1.2–1.8 ms after the thalamic spike. In cat V1 silenced with muscimol, geniculate afferent spikes can be recorded as units throughout layer 4 (182 afferents mapped), so thalamocortical axons are an extracellularly recordable spike source in L4. In rat, the earliest component of the thalamically evoked cortical field (1.0–1.6 ms after VPL stimulation) was attributed to presynaptic thalamocortical fibers and was unrelated to cortical cell discharges. I found no study that quantifies what fraction of L4 MUA (thresholded or envelope) comes from thalamic afferents.

### Cited Findings
- **Swadlow, Gusev & Bezdudnaya 2002 (awake rabbit S1; VB/VPM thalamocortical spike-triggered averages and CSD).**
  - Responses consist of an "initial biphasic or triphasic 'axon-terminal' component" with latency <1 ms, then a negative-going potential "usually 1.2–1.8 msec" after the spike, interpreted as "an extracellular current sink generated by monosynaptic TC activation." Supporting evidence is AMPA/kainate antagonist block and restriction to one barrel, from prior work.
  - In the example neuron, "the axon-terminal potential had a latency of 0.81 msec" and was isolated from the postsynaptic current sink, "with an onset latency of 1.58 msec."
  - Mean aTP-to-sink-onset interval: 0.72 ms in layer 4 and 0.68 ms in layer 6.
  - Of 24 TC neurons, 14 produced sinks limited to layer 4, 9 produced sinks in layers 4 and 6, and 1 produced a sink only in layer 6.
  - The authors note that "local action potentials undoubtedly made some contribution to the observed postsynaptic current sinks."
  - — [J Neurosci 22:7766–7773, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.22-17-07766.2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6757983)
- **Stoelzel, Bereshpolova, Gusev & Swadlow 2008 (awake rabbit V1; LGNd spike-triggered CSD).**
  - The aTP is a "biphasic or triphasic response (usually initially positive-going)" with rise time about 0.2 ms.
  - The postsynaptic sink follows "the onset of the axonal potential by <1 ms," with a 10–90% rise time of about 0.45 ms.
  - Presynaptic and postsynaptic components separate pharmacologically by interval dependence: "the axon terminal potential is not affected by this variable [preceding interspike interval]," whereas "the postsynaptic component clearly is" (synaptic depression).
  - Axonal conduction times averaged 0.96 ms for sustained and 0.69 ms for transient cells. "Seventy of the 81 LGNd neurons… generated a presynaptic and postsynaptic response in layer 4."
  - — [J Neurosci 28:5018, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.4726-07.2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713607/)
- **Jin et al. 2008 (cat V1; Alonso/Swadlow labs).**
  - Spike-triggered CSD of single geniculate neurons separated a "fast axonal component (0.8–2.1 ms after the geniculate spike)" from a postsynaptic sink, defined as the first 1 ms after a "synaptic delay (~0.5 ms)."
  - The axonal component "had a triphasic time-course that corresponded to the spike recorded from the geniculate axonal terminal within the cortex."
  - Sinks were "focused within layer 4 (and sometimes also in layer 6)."
  - In a separate arm, 50 mM muscimol perfused on the pia for about 2 h left "the cortical cells… no longer active." Geniculate afferents were then recorded throughout layer 4 and identified "by their ability to follow rapid geniculate stimulation." In total, 182 afferents were mapped in 14 penetrations (7–23 per penetration).
  - — [Nat Neurosci 11:88–94 (PMC2556869)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556869)
- **Hagen et al. 2017 (biophysical model of spike-triggered LFP and CSD; data from rabbit S1/V1 and cat V1).**
  - The postsynaptic stLFP is preceded by "fast transients caused by presynaptic action-potential propagation in the thalamic afferent" (Fig. 4A). "These presynaptic LFP components were not included in the model because the transmembrane currents in the axonal boutons were not included."
  - With an imposed 1.4 ms synaptic delay, the model CSD minimum was at 1.89 ± 0.05 ms for the FS population and 3.10 ± 0.03 ms for the RS population. "The rapid onset of the stLFP is provided by contributions from the FS cells," and "the RS cells contributed most of the latter part."
  - "The LFP is more closely related to the synaptically driven transmembrane currents than to the membrane potential."
  - Experimental stLFPs used spontaneous TC spikes that "in general did not drive the postsynaptic cells to fire action potentials."
  - — [J Neurosci 37:5123–5143, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.2715-16.2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5444196/)
- **Kandel & Buzsáki 1997 (rat S1; VPL stimulation; silicon probes, CSD plus units).**
  - The earliest component of the cortical response had a latency of 1.0–1.6 ms. Unit recordings showed "no relationship between cell discharges and this earliest component in deep layers of the neocortex," and the authors suggest it reflects "synchronous discharge of the presynaptic thalamocortical fibers."
  - "The sinks in layers V–VI occurred simultaneously or even slightly earlier than the sink in layer IV."
  - Thalamocortical synapses "comprise only 20% of synapses in this layer," and "the major current flow derives from the activity of the intracortical circuitry."
  - — [J Neurosci 17:6783–6797, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.17-17-06783.1997](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6573130)
- **Constantinople & Bruno 2013 (rat barrel cortex, lightly sedated; whole-cell recordings).**
  - Whisker-evoked PSP onset latencies: L4 7.76 ± 0.16 ms (n = 24), L5 9.44 ± 0.3 ms (n = 53), L6 10.68 ± 0.67 ms (n = 13), L2/3 11.04 ± 0.26 ms (n = 18). Many L5 cells "rivaled L4 in latency."
  - Unitary VPM→L5/6 connections had onset latency 2.40 ± 0.31 ms. Lidocaine silencing of L4 (L4 LFP fell from 0.69 to 0.21 mV) left L5/6 PSPs unchanged (7.53 vs 7.58 mV).
  - — [Science 340:1591–1594 (PMC4203320)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4203320)
- **Einevoll et al. 2007 (laminar population analysis, rat barrel cortex).**
  - "The high-frequency band [multiunit activity (MUA)] is modeled as a sum over contributions from firing activity of multiple cortical populations, whereas the low-frequency band [LFP] is assumed to reflect the dendritic currents caused by synaptic inputs evoked by this firing."
  - In other words, the method assumes MUA comes from cortical populations. The abstract does not model a thalamic-axon MUA contribution.
  - — [J Neurophysiol 97:2174–2190, DOI 10.1152/jn.00845.2006](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00845.2006)
- **Blomquist et al. 2009 (rat barrel system; joint VPM single-electrode and cortical laminar recordings).**
  - Thalamic population firing was estimated from thalamic MUA. Fitted thalamocortical "feedforward time constants are between 3 and 6 milliseconds and the feedforward delay between 2 and 3 milliseconds." Recurrent excitation time constants were 8–10 ms and recurrent inhibition 13–15 ms.
  - "The estimated thalamic population firing rate is essentially just a low-pass filtered version of the thalamic MUA."
  - — [PLoS Comput Biol 5:e1000328 (PMC2653726)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2653726/)
- **Sakata & Harris 2009 (rat A1, urethane; replicated in unanesthetized rats).**
  - "For evoked responses, activity started in putative L4 and the L5/6 border." These locations "correspond on an experiment-to-experiment basis with the locations of early sinks measured by CSD analysis."
  - Spontaneous upstates start in deep layers. Peak latency was "defined as median MUA spike time in a 50ms window after event onset."
  - — [Neuron 64:404–418 (PMC2778614)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2778614)
- **Bruno & Sakmann 2006.** "Cortex is driven by weak but synchronously active thalamocortical synapses," *Science* 312:1622–1627. My recollection is that it reports small unitary VPM→L4 EPSPs (about 0.5 mV), with dozens of convergent inputs, so cortical spiking depends on thalamic synchrony. **[unverified — not read this session]** — [DOI 10.1126/science.1124593](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1124593)
- **Jellema, Brunia & Wadman 2004 (rat S1; median-nerve SEP; 12-channel CSD, ketamine).** The abstract says "Axon-collaterals of layer Vb pyramidal cells produce an enhanced activation of the supragranular pyramidal cells in layer I–II, which generates N1." Latencies were not retrieved. — [Neuroscience 129:283–295, DOI 10.1016/j.neuroscience.2004.07.046](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2004.07.046)

### Inferences
- Thalamocortical axon and terminal spikes are recordable by extracellular electrodes in L4. Jin et al. 2008 isolated them as units after silencing cortex, and their field signature (aTP) leads the monosynaptic sink by about 0.5–0.7 ms in single-axon averages. Any high-pass MUA measure in L4 can therefore contain afferent spikes, and those spikes would precede the postsynaptic sink they cause by roughly a synaptic delay plus rise time (about 0.5–1 ms). This is an inference from the single-axon work; no study quantified it for population MUA.
- How large the afferent share of MUA is depends on how many TC axons and boutons lie within the electrode's "listening" radius and on their spike amplitudes compared with cortical somata. Neither quantity was measured in the sources found. Thalamocortical synapses are a minority of L4 synapses: about 20% according to Kandel & Buzsáki 1997, and "~10–20%" according to Happel et al. 2010 (see Q3). Somatic spikes of L4 neurons are probably the larger MUA source once the cortex is active, but this was not measured.
- The deep-layer (L5/6) monosynaptic input reported in rat S1 and A1 (Kandel & Buzsáki; Constantinople & Bruno; Sakata & Harris) means "earliest spikes" in a laminar MUA profile may lie at the L5/6 border rather than in L4.

### Gaps
- No study found reports the fraction of L4 MUA power or threshold crossings attributable to thalamic afferents in active (non-silenced) cortex, in any species.
- Swadlow & Gusev 2000 (J Neurophysiol, rabbit barrel cortex, which defined the aTP), Castro-Alamancos (thalamocortical field potentials in barrel cortex), Pettersen et al. 2008 (J Comput Neurosci, CSD and firing-rate estimation), and Tenke et al. 1993 (Exp Brain Res, a simulation of sublaminar terminal contributions) were not retrieved. Their specific statements remain unverified.

## Q3. Rodent and cat auditory and visual laminar recordings: does the L4 sink or L4 MUA/spiking come first, and by how many ms?

### Takeaway
I found no non-primate paper that reports a direct, quantified onset (or half-max or peak) comparison between the L4 CSD sink and L4 MUA in the same recordings. The available evidence is qualitative or indirect:
- Earliest MUA sites coincide with early-sink sites (rat A1).
- The earliest evoked field component (about 1–1.6 ms after thalamic shock) is presynaptic and unaccompanied by cortical unit firing (rat S1).
- The early granular sink survives cortical silencing with unchanged onset but loses about 88% of its peak amplitude (gerbil A1).
- Deep-layer (L5/6) sinks can be as early as, or earlier than, the L4 sink (rat S1, rat A1, mouse A1).

### Cited Findings
- **Happel, Jeschke & Ohl 2010 (gerbil A1; tone-evoked CSD; cortical muscimol silencing).**
  - For best-frequency tones, "neither its onset latency nor its laminar profile was affected by cortical silencing" for the granular sink S1, and onset latencies "did not show significant changes" (p = 0.92).
  - S1 peak amplitude fell about 88% (−1.111 to −0.134 mV/mm²). "Cortical silencing blocked longer-latency extragranular sinks."
  - Deeper layer IV/Va responses became "faster (1–2 ms)" after silencing.
  - Thalamic input is "only ~10–20% of inputs to cortical layer IV." MUA latency relative to sinks was not reported.
  - — [J Neurosci 30:11114, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.0689-10.2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6633479/)
- **Intskirveli et al. 2016 (rodent A1; "Spectral breadth and laminar distribution of thalamocortical inputs to A1").** 100 µM cortical muscimol "strongly reduced intracortical activity, as reflected in MUA," while the earliest L4 sink (monosynaptic thalamocortical input) appeared intact. L5/6 responses were unaffected. **[snippet — full text returned 403]** — [J Neurophysiol, DOI 10.1152/jn.00887.2015](https://journals.physiology.org/doi/full/10.1152/jn.00887.2015)
- **Szymanski et al. 2011 (rat A1; ketamine/medetomidine).**
  - In an example penetration, "the earliest sink arises at a depth of ~1200 µm from the surface" about "~10 ms after stimulus onset," and "is followed almost immediately by a much stronger current sink at 400–500 µm" (granular/lower III).
  - "Responses with very short latency are found not only in the chief thalamo-recipient layers… but also in the deepest cortical layers."
  - For automated detection, CSD event onset was set "19 ms before this peak, based on the typical latencies between the first appearance of the current sink and the peak of the convolution." This illustrates a long onset-to-peak span for sinks. No MUA latency was compared.
  - — [J Neurosci 31:15787–15801, DOI 10.1523/JNEUROSCI.1416-11.2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6623019/)
- **Mouse A1/A2 (2025, Nature Communications).** "Short-latency CSD sinks were observed in L6a of both A1 and A2, which were significantly faster than those in L4." The paper compared CSD and multiunit latencies across layers. **[snippet — numbers not read]** — [Nat Commun 2025, s41467-025-61020-9](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-025-61020-9); [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.01.06.631598v2.full)
- **Rodent A1, thalamic stimulation (Front Syst Neurosci 2014, probably Krause et al.).** Early current sinks are located in layer 4 and deep layer 3. When the CSD showed weak UP states, MUA was seen in L4 and L5 but very little in L2/3. Spiking was "dominated by cortical network activity." **[snippet; authorship not confirmed]** — [PubMed 25285071](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25285071/); [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/systems-neuroscience/articles/10.3389/fnsys.2014.00170/full)
- **Senzai, Fernandez-Ruiz & Buzsáki 2019 (awake mouse V1).**
  - Visual stimulation produced "an early sink (b′) at the border between layers 3 and 4," which "likely reflected afferent activation from the lateral geniculate body," together with a sink-source pair at the L5/6 border.
  - For the spontaneous 3–6 Hz waking rhythm (not evoked responses): "The earliest and strongest firing was present in layers 4 and 5B, coinciding with the LFP sink in layer 4."
  - No evoked sink-versus-MUA latencies were found in the text read.
  - — [Neuron 101:500–513, DOI 10.1016/j.neuron.2018.12.009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6367010)
- **Rimehaug et al. 2023 (biophysical model of mouse V1 fitted to Allen Neuropixels flash responses).**
  - "An early transient sink arising in layer 4 (L4) ~40 ms after flash onset." "The L4 sink is primarily generated by inflowing current thalamocortical synapses onto L4 excitatory cells," mostly at basal and apical dendrites.
  - The CSD rise at 40–50 ms is "compatible with the latency of spiking responses to full-field flashes in V1."
  - For the later L5/L6 sink, removing recurrent connections "substantially diminished" it, "indicat[ing] that recurrent synapses also contribute significantly."
  - "The somatic voltage-dependent membrane currents significantly shape the CSD signature."
  - — [eLife 12:e87169, DOI 10.7554/eLife.87169](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.87169)
- **Kandel & Buzsáki 1997 (rat S1).** The presynaptic earliest component (1.0–1.6 ms after VPL stimulation) shows "no relationship" with cortical cell discharges. L5–VI sinks occurred "simultaneously or even slightly earlier" than the L4 sink. See Q2. — [PMC6573130](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6573130)
- **Sakata & Harris 2009 (rat A1).** Evoked MUA starts in putative L4 and at the L5/6 border, the same sites as the early CSD sinks. Sink-versus-spike latency was not quantified in the text read. See Q2. — [PMC2778614](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2778614)
- **Constantinople & Bruno 2013 (rat S1).** L4 PSP onset 7.76 ms versus L5 9.44 ms. This is subthreshold onset, not CSD. See Q2. — [PMC4203320](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4203320)
- **Kaur, Lazar & Metherate 2004 (rat A1; tone CSD plus whole-cell).** The study computed 1-D CSD with a three-point formula. A later paper cites it for "the problem of accurate onset and offset latency determination." No numbers were retrieved. — [J Neurophysiol 91:2551–2567, DOI 10.1152/jn.01121.2003](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.01121.2003)
- **Mitzdorf 1985 (cat 17/18).** Early layer IV/VI sinks precede the layer III sink. Brighter stimuli shorten latency and compress the CSD. No CSD-versus-MUA onset comparison was made. See Q1. — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)

### Inferences
- The silencing data (Happel 2010; Intskirveli 2016 [snippet]) imply that the earliest part of the granular sink is thalamocortical and present without cortical spiking. Most of its peak amplitude (about 88% in gerbil) requires intracortical activity. Sink onset therefore indexes afferent input, while sink peak and half-max index the recurrent, spike-driven build-up. Comparing MUA half-max or peak with sink half-max or peak thus compares a spike measure against a mostly intracortical current, and it can come out "spikes first" without contradicting the canonical causal order.
- Rat S1 and A1 and mouse A1 all show short-latency deep (L5/6, L6a) sinks or firing. An analysis that takes the "earliest sink" or "earliest MUA" from the whole laminar profile may compare different layers.

### Gaps
- No cat or rodent study was found that reports L4 sink onset versus L4 MUA onset, in ms, from the same electrodes after natural stimulation. Candidate sources not retrieved include Kaur et al. 2004/2005, Szymanski et al. 2009 (J Neurophysiol, SSA CSD plus MUA), Di, Baumgartner & Barth 1990 (rat barrel CSD), Jellema 2004 full text, the 2025 Nat Commun A1/A2 paper (full text), and Intskirveli 2016 (full text).

## Q4. General expectations: afferent spikes → sink → postsynaptic spikes? How the sink's slow build-up affects half-max and peak comparisons

### Takeaway
At the level of a single thalamocortical axon, the order is well established in rabbit and cat:
- the presynaptic impulse and terminal potential (≤1 ms after the thalamic spike);
- the monosynaptic sink, starting about 0.5–0.7 ms later, with FS-cell currents contributing earliest;
- postsynaptic spikes, which require convergent, synchronous input.

Theory (Mitzdorf; Buzsáki et al.; Einevoll et al.) holds that CSD sinks reflect synaptic currents and that spikes contribute little under dispersed natural activation. Because the population sink keeps growing through recurrent excitation (fitted time constants 8–10 ms versus 3–6 ms for feedforward thalamic input in rat barrel cortex, Blomquist 2009) and most of its amplitude is intracortical, its half-max and peak can occur later than the half-max of a steeply rising spike measure. Its onset should still follow afferent arrival.

### Cited Findings
- **Synaptic current dominates the field.** "The major contributor of the extracellular signal is the synaptic transmembrane current." Fast Na+ spikes "generate the strongest currents across the neuronal membrane," and "synchronous action potentials from many neurons can contribute substantially to high-frequency components of the LFP… increased power in the higher-frequency bands can be regarded as an index of spiking synchrony." — [Buzsáki, Anastassiou & Koch 2012, Nat Rev Neurosci 13:407–420, DOI 10.1038/nrn3241](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4907333/)
- **CSD caveats.** It is "not possible to conclude using CSD measurement alone whether" a current near the soma layer reflects active inhibition or "the passive return current of active excitatory currents," and "ambiguity may still remain if the sinks and sources are generated by a non-synaptic mechanism." — [Buzsáki et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/4907333/)
- **Mitzdorf's estimates.** EPSPs produce 2–3× larger CSDs than fiber or somatic APs. Somatic APs are "totally negligible" during natural activation because biphasic currents cancel when latency scatter is ≥1.5 ms. Synaptic and terminal CSDs are monophasic and do not cancel with temporal scatter; they only broaden. The capacitive current tracks the slope of the PSP, and "no net membrane current flows during this decay phase." — [Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)
- **Single-axon timing.** Rabbit S1: aTP at about 0.8 ms, sink onset 1.2–1.8 ms after the thalamic spike, aTP-to-sink 0.72 ms ([Swadlow et al. 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6757983)). Rabbit V1: sink follows aTP by <1 ms ([Stoelzel et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713607/)). Cat V1: axonal component 0.8–2.1 ms, synaptic delay about 0.5 ms ([Jin et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556869)).
- **Cell-type order within the monosynaptic sink (model).** The FS population's CSD peak (1.89 ms) precedes the RS peak (3.10 ms) by about 1.2 ms with the same 1.4 ms synaptic delay. The difference is my calculation from the reported means. "The rapid onset of the stLFP is provided by contributions from the FS cells." — [Hagen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5444196/)
- **Feedforward versus recurrent time constants (rat barrel, fitted).** Thalamocortical feedforward time constants 3–6 ms with a 2–3 ms delay; recurrent excitation 8–10 ms; recurrent inhibition 13–15 ms. The model predicts "the maximum in the layer-4 responses will precede the maximum of the thalamic input" at the lowest frequencies, a phase property of the fitted transfer function. — [Blomquist et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2653726/)
- **Most of the L4 sink amplitude is intracortical.** Gerbil A1: muscimol cut the granular S1 peak by about 88% with onset unchanged ([Happel et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6633479/)). Rat S1: "the major current flow derives from the activity of the intracortical circuitry," with TC synapses about 20% of L4 synapses ([Kandel & Buzsáki 1997](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6573130)). Mouse V1 model: by contrast, the early L4 sink is "primarily generated by… thalamocortical synapses," and recurrent synapses contribute significantly to the later L5/6 sink ([Rimehaug et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.87169)).
- **Postsynaptic spikes need convergent synchrony.** Single spontaneous TC spikes "in general did not drive the postsynaptic cells to fire action potentials" ([Hagen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5444196/)). Bruno & Sakmann 2006 is the usual citation for weak but synchronous TC synapses **[unverified this session]** ([DOI 10.1126/science.1124593](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1124593)).
- **Population MUA and the LFP are modelled as cortical.** In laminar population analysis, MUA is modelled as cortical population firing, and the LFP as dendritic synaptic currents "evoked by this firing." — [Einevoll et al. 2007](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00845.2006)

### Inferences
- **Onset order expected from mechanism (my synthesis of the cited findings).**
  - Afferent axon and terminal spikes come first. They are recordable in L4 and appear in high-pass MUA.
  - The monosynaptic sink starts about 0.5–1 ms later.
  - FS interneuron spikes come next, then RS/stellate spikes, which need EPSP summation.
  - Postsynaptic L4 MUA onset should therefore follow sink onset by at least the charging time to threshold. Afferent-driven MUA, however, can lead or coincide with sink onset.
  - Under visual stimulation, LGN latencies are dispersed over many ms, so the afferent and postsynaptic distributions overlap. Onset differences of about 1 ms are unlikely to be resolvable in trial-averaged population signals. This expectation is not measured in any source found.
- **Why half-max and peak can invert the order.**
  1. The population sink amplitude is dominated by intracortical, spike-driven currents (about 88% in gerbil A1), and these build on recurrent time constants (about 8–10 ms) slower than the feedforward drive.
  2. CSD is a linear sum of synaptic currents, while MUA is a thresholded, nonlinear measure that can saturate early. Spikes from TC axons and FS cells front-load it.
  3. Detection on noisy CSD (lower SNR, spatial smoothing) tends to delay apparent sink onset relative to threshold-crossing MUA.

  A finding that L4 MUA half-max precedes L4 sink half-max is therefore not evidence against the input → sink → spikes order. Comparing sink onset (or initial slope) with MUA onset is the appropriate test. Even so, a "spikes-before-sink" result of ≤1 ms could reflect afferent spikes in the MUA.
- **Polarity of the deep-layer confound.** Short-latency sinks and spikes at L5/6 (rat S1 and A1, mouse A1) mean that the deep layers, not only L4, can carry the earliest events.

### Gaps
- I found no non-primate empirical study that reports half-max or peak timing of the L4 sink alongside L4 MUA from the same contacts. The inversion argument above is an inference.
- I found no quantitative estimate of the share of population L4 MUA from thalamocortical axons in intact cortex. Einevoll et al. 2013 (Nat Rev Neurosci, "Modelling and analysis of local field potentials for studying the function of cortical circuits") and Pettersen et al. 2008 could hold relevant modelling statements, but neither was retrieved.
- The 1985 assumption that spikes do not invade terminals conflicts with the later "terminal spike" description (Jin 2008). Modern bouton physiology was not reviewed here.
