# Laminar timing can locate interocular normalization in V1

Primate V1 combines the two eyes' contrasts almost linearly near threshold and normalizes them above it, but nobody has settled where, when or with what weighting that normalization happens. Near threshold, single neurons in anesthetized macaque add left- and right-eye contrast linearly, and human detection improves 1.5–1.7× with two eyes. Above threshold, awake-macaque multiunit responses to matched gratings are only **~56% of the summed monocular responses**. Superficial populations respond about equally to one eye or two. Human BOLD and SSVEP show near-complete ocularity invariance, which only models in which each eye divides the other's gain (interocular weight w ≈ 1) can fit. The disputes are about architecture. Cat simple cells put gain control at a monocular stage, while human population signals need a binocular pool. Interocular suppression has both a fast precortical route and a slow, GABAergic cortical one. Ocular dominance (OD) is treated variously as a weight on excitation, a weight on the normalization pool, or irrelevant. No study has combined laminar resolution, millisecond timing, isolated units and a full dichoptic contrast matrix, and human laminar fMRI cannot localize the effect because of its superficial vascular bias. That leaves four things the team's dataset can add that Mitchell et al. 2022/2023 could not. The first is the **onset latency of interocular suppression by depth and other-eye contrast**, which the corrected stimulus clock makes measurable for the first time. The second is the **equivalent-contrast factor k**, defined by R_mono(k·c) = R(c,c) and compared between transient and sustained epochs; it separates linear summation (k = 2), normalization before summation (k ≈ 1.4–1.5) and contrast-weighted averaging (k → 1). The third is the **depth profile of w**. The fourth is whether normalization already appears in the **earliest current sink** or only in spiking. Orientation conflict, disparity, temporal frequency, adaptation and monoptic masking lie outside the design, and supersaturation is already retired.

## Two eyes add near threshold and normalize above it

Preparations are tagged inline as mac-W (awake, fixating macaque), mac-A (anesthetized, paralyzed macaque), cat-A (anesthetized, paralyzed cat), human, mouse or marmoset, and "preprint" marks work that is not peer reviewed. A statement marked **derived** is algebra or simulation from published equations, done by the research team or for this report. It is not a published result.

### Saturation is real, supersaturation is absent, and laminar CRF parameters are unpublished

The monocular contrast response function (CRF) keeps its shape through the response. In anesthetized monkeys and cats, contrast normalization and expansive half-squaring are **fully expressed within the first 10 ms**, and one Naka–Rushton fit with fixed c50 and exponent describes the first through the last 10 ms of a 200 ms response ([Albrecht et al. 2002 · mac-A, cat-A](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12163540/)). CRFs steepen from LGN to V1 to MT ([Sclar et al. 1990 · mac-A](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(90)90123-3)). Saturation at the top of the grating range partly reflects the limit of the stimulus. With Gaussian increments up to 900% Weber contrast, average awake responses keep rising above 100%, and only a small minority of neurons saturate below it ([Yoshida et al. 2026 · mac-W, preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.06.15.732419v1)). Rmax at Michelson 0.9 can be estimated, but it is a ceiling of the stimulus range rather than of the neuron.

Laminar contrast evidence is old and confined to layer 4 and 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG). Magno-recipient 4Cα holds high-sensitivity neurons and parvo-recipient 4Cβ low-sensitivity ones ([Hawken & Parker 1984 · mac-A](https://doi.org/10.1007/bf00236238)). Layers 4A, 4Cα and 4Cβ are "populated mostly by monocularly driven cells having small receptive fields and lacking orientation selectivity," and 4Cα has the lower thresholds ([Blasdel & Fitzpatrick 1984 · mac-A](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.04-03-00880.1984)). At 8% contrast, 2-DG labels 4Cα but not 4Cβ. Uptake saturates at ≥50%, with 4B, 4Cα and 6 most active ([Tootell et al. 1988 · mac, 2-DG](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6569196)). **No study reports Naka–Rushton c50, n and Rmax layer by layer across V1**, awake or anesthetized.

The team's rebuilt data already answer the saturation questions (team data, unpublished). There is no supersaturation at any depth (MUA well powered, single units at chance). Saturation is slightly weaker with depth. Dioptic stimuli show no extra supersaturation. The literature does not predict that depth trend uniquely. A magno/parvo account expects early saturation in 4Cα, 4B and 6, and layer 6 behaves like magnocellular 4Cα in its suppression profile ([Henry et al. 2020 · mac-A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7253173/)). Weaker saturation at the deepest contacts is therefore a new observation rather than a confirmation.

**Near threshold the eyes combine linearly; above it they combine strongly sublinearly.** Smith and colleagues varied left:right contrast ratios from 0.1 to 10. For all cell classes and both cooperative and antagonistic phase relations, they found "a linear relationship between the left- and right-eye contrast components required to produce a threshold response" ([Smith, Chino, Ni & Cheng 1997 · mac-A](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1997.78.1.366)). Cat neurometrics show lower binocular thresholds and steeper slopes, but "no single rule" across cells ([Anzai et al. 1995 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0952523800007331)).

Above threshold, Mitchell et al. used arrays in two other monkeys (E and I) at about 20%, 45% and 90% contrast ([Mitchell et al. 2022 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2022.104182)). Dioptic responses were **0.55–0.57 times the summed monocular responses at every contrast**, and 69.7% of units were facilitated. Facilitation scaled with contrast in the transient. Later it persisted at low contrast but turned into suppression at high contrast. Response-gain fits beat contrast-gain fits by **small margins**: R² 0.90 vs 0.87 at 50–150 ms, and 0.81 vs 0.73 at 150–250 ms.

Two-photon imaging of 9,390 tuned superficial neurons at 0.9 contrast found an overall binocular response "similar to that with monocular viewing" ([Zhang et al. 2024 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.92839)). On this same dataset, the non-dominant eye's CRF was best described by changing only Rmax relative to the dominant eye (adj. R² 0.92), not c50 (0.82) or the exponent (0.79) ([Mitchell et al. 2023 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2023.08.019)). Per-neuron changes in the Naka–Rushton exponent between monocular and dioptic stimulation are **unreported in awake macaque**.

One derivation sharpens the stakes. Take single-stage interocular normalization with equal eye weights, R = (cLⁿ + cRⁿ)/(cLⁿ + cRⁿ + c50ⁿ). Its dioptic CRF is the monocular CRF shifted left by exactly 2^(1/n), with Rmax unchanged (derived). That is a pure contrast-gain change, so any Rmax change has to come from elsewhere.

Now give each eye its own excitatory weight (a_D, a_N) and let w weight the other eye's contribution to the pool, as in Moradi–Heeger Eq. 10. The saturated dioptic response, relative to the dominant eye's monocular response, is then **(1 + a_N/a_D)/(1 + w)** (derived for this report). Rmax rises only when a_N/a_D exceeds w, and falls when it lies below. On that reading, Mitchell 2022's transient facilitation and late response-gain suppression at high contrast look like w growing over the response. That interpretation is derived, and depth- and epoch-resolved fits can test it.

### Two eyes beat one by 1.5–1.7× at threshold and by almost nothing above it

Human detection thresholds show more summation than classical theory allows. Legge's energy model squares each eye's signal before summing, which predicts a ratio of √2 ([Legge 1984b · human](https://legge.psych.umn.edu/sites/legge.psych.umn.edu/files/files/media/legge84_binocular_contrast_summation._ii._quadratic_summation.pdf)). Meese, Georgeson & Baker measured **1.70 (4.6 dB)**, between √2 and linear summation ([Meese et al. 2006 · human](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/1422/1/jov_6_11_7.pdf)). They also showed that probability summation (≤1.5 dB) and ideal-observer combination (~1.2 dB with accelerating transduction) cannot produce that much, so the summation is neural.

A meta-analysis of 65 studies (N = 716) put the ratio at **1.53 unweighted, 1.50 sample-size-weighted and 1.47 inverse-variance-weighted**, all above √2 ([Baker et al. 2018 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6195301/)). Summation fell with stimulus speed, following m = 1.14 + 0.28·log10(TF/SF) (R² = 0.70). It also fell by 0.3–0.5 dB for each 1 dB of interocular sensitivity imbalance. The authors conclude that "there is no single value for binocular summation."

Above threshold, binocularity stops raising discrimination or perceived contrast, and dichoptic masks hit hardest. Peak dichoptic facilitation in the dipper was **4.1 dB**, against 9.9 dB monocular and 8.0 dB binocular. Yet with a pedestal in both eyes and the test in one, summation persisted at 5.1 dB. Suprathreshold summation is therefore hidden by the measurement, not abolished ([Meese et al. 2006](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/1422/1/jov_6_11_7.pdf)). On a 5×5 L×R grid (0.3–32%), the two-stage model predicted both discrimination and matching with no free parameters. Once one eye reached 32%, perceived contrast became "essentially independent" of the other ([Baker, Meese & Georgeson 2007 · human](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/1426/1/AURA1426.pdf)).

Ocularity invariance is not universal. For luminance flicker, cortical EEG combines the eyes almost linearly (**w ≈ 0.02**), against w ≈ 0.61 for pupillometry and 0.09 for perceptual matching. The authors conclude that "ocularity invariance is, therefore, not a ubiquitous feature of visual processing" ([Segala et al. 2023 · human](https://elifesciences.org/articles/87048)).

These benchmarks translate into concrete expectations for neurons. Monocular normalization followed by linear summation (w = 0) predicts a dioptic response equal to the summed monocular responses, so Mitchell 2022's 0.56 slope already excludes w = 0 for pooled awake MUA (derived). Once responses pass c50, every normalizing model predicts a dioptic-to-summed fraction near 0.5–0.6 that barely changes across 20–90%. The discriminating information therefore sits in the 5.5–22.5% cells (derived). Neural c50s typically sit far above the BOLD semisaturation of 3.6% ([Moradi & Heeger 2009 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747122/)). Neurons should therefore show larger dioptic-to-monocular ratios across more of the grid than BOLD fits imply (inference in the notes). And because sensitivity imbalance alone erodes summation, neural ratios should be computed after equating each eye's sensitivity, before they are compared with psychophysics.

### Interocular suppression has a fast precortical route and a slower cortical one

In cat, monoptic and dichoptic suppression are different mechanisms. Binocular cross-orientation suppression is "primarily monocular and acts prior to the convergence of the two monocular streams," and dichoptic effects are weak but "present in almost every cell" ([Walker, Ohzawa & Freeman 1998 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1998.79.1.227)). Monoptic suppression survives masks that drift too fast to drive cortex and is immune to adaptation of mask-driven neurons, which places it subcortically ([Freeman et al. 2002 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0896-6273(02)00819-x)). LGN saturation and rectification in a feedforward model reproduce it ([Priebe & Ferster 2006 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1038/nn1660)).

Dichoptic suppression behaves differently. It prefers low temporal frequency and is reduced by prolonged adaptation ([Li et al. 2005 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00203.2005)). Interocular suppression "cannot be evoked by very fast-moving stimuli," is essentially eliminated by adaptation, and "is greatly reduced in the presence of the GABA antagonist bicuculline" ([Sengpiel & Vorobyov 2005 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.0862-05.2005)). It is also nonlinear: it matters only when the cell is already responding. Meanwhile almost half of LGN neurons show modest, orientation-independent interocular inhibition ([Sengpiel, Blakemore & Harrad 1995 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(94)00125-6)).

Truchard et al. held one eye fixed and varied the other over a tenfold range ([Truchard et al. 2000 · cat-A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6772209/)). **Monocular gain fell about 5× while binocular gain changed about 1.2×** (slopes −0.64 vs −0.05). They placed gain control "primarily at a monocular site" that "might arise at a subcortical site," and they did not study transient dynamics.

Human psychophysics shows the same split. Ipsiocular suppression is broadband and resists adaptation, while interocular suppression is tuned, adaptable and grows with stimulus duration ([Baker, Meese & Summers 2007 · human](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2007.01.030)). Monocular masking weight depends on speed, whereas dichoptic masking does not ([Meese & Baker 2009 · human](https://doi.org/10.1167/9.5.2)).

Primates add a precortical component. In awake LGN, P, M and K units "were significantly suppressed when stimuli were presented at low contrast to the dominant eye and at high contrast to the non-dominant eye" ([Dougherty et al. 2021 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1523/eneuro.0364-20.2020)). Flash-evoked LGN multiunit activity falls 15–70% with binocular stimulation. Some of these effects appear at 15–65 ms, before the earliest V1 layer-6 response, so they cannot come from cortical feedback ([Schroeder et al. 1990 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1016/0006-8993(90)91556-v)).

In V1, one awake study dissents. It found neurons "dichoptic in terms of excitation, but monoptic in terms of inhibition" ([Macknik & Martinez-Conde 2004 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1162/0898929041502788)). Most others find substantial dichoptic suppression. Orthogonal dichoptic gratings suppress sustained responses in more than half of V1 neurons by about 10 spikes/s ([Cox et al. 2019 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1167/19.12.13)). Continuous flash suppression "nearly abolish[ed] responses in neurons preferring the masker eye or both eyes" ([Chen et al. 2026 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.107518)). Fellow-eye noise masks attenuate responses in normal control animals ([Shooner et al. 2017 · mac-A](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.1760-17.2017)).

Suppression runs on at least two clocks. On the fast clock, cross-orientation suppression begins at 42.5 ms against 50.0 ms for excitation, too early for feedback ([Smith, Bair & Movshon 2006 · mac-A](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6674161)). Full-field surround suppression appears in layer 4C at response onset ([Bijanzadeh et al. 2018 · mac-A](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10110109)). Disparity-specific binocular suppression lags excitation by a **median of 7 ms** ([Tanabe & Cumming 2014 · mac-W](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4044366/)). In awake laminar MUA at 90% contrast, fast suppression follows excitation by 11–21 ms and is strongest in the input layers (4C, 6). Slow suppression follows at 45–61 ms and is about 3× stronger in the output layers (2/3, 4B, 5) ([Wang et al. 2020 · mac-W](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7511183)).

The cat signature of interocular suppression (low temporal frequency, adaptable, GABAergic) belongs to the slow, cortical class. The primate LGN, however, shows that some interocular suppression already rides in on the thalamic input. Which source dominates at each V1 depth and latency is unmeasured: **no published study gives a per-layer onset latency of interocular suppression in macaque V1.** The team's iso-oriented gratings probe the strongest case. Same-orientation interocular suppression in human V1 BOLD was about **twice** orthogonal suppression (fitted w = 1 vs 0.52) ([Moradi & Heeger 2009 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747122/)).

### Ocular dominance weights excitation, but its effect on the normalization pool is untested

The cat and stereo literature treats OD as largely irrelevant to binocular interaction. Binocular interaction "does not depend critically on the ocular dominance" ([Ohzawa & Freeman 1986a · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1986.56.1.221)). A cell dominated by one eye "may respond equally under binocular conditions" ([Freeman & Ohzawa 1990 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1016/0042-6989(90)90151-a)). OD did not correlate with gain slopes ([Truchard et al. 2000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6772209/)). OD "predicts neither strength nor class of disparity selectivity" ([Read & Cumming 2004 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00588.2003)).

Recent awake work says OD matters. On this dataset (502 multiunits, 50–200 ms), an OD weight derived only from monocular data improved every binocular model (p < 0.00001) ([Mitchell et al. 2023 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2023.08.019)). It improved interocular normalization most (AIC +21.5%), and absolute OD predicted binocular response asymmetry (adj. R² = 0.46). In two-photon imaging, binocular modulation scales with absolute ODI (slope −0.41), so binocular responses are nearly flat across OD ([Zhang et al. 2024 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.92839)). In amblyopic macaques, excitation is dominated by the fellow eye, yet "suppression from both the FE and AEs was prevalent in all animals" ([Hallum et al. 2017 · mac-A](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.0449-17.2017)). A re-analysis of cat adaptation data favours a model in which "signals driving IOS arise 'early', before attenuation due to OD, while suppressive CGC signals are 'late' and so affected by OD" ([Georgeson & Sengpiel 2021 · cat-A, model](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.visres.2021.03.004)).

The camps can be reconciled by asking where OD enters the normalization equation. The three variants below make different predictions, all derived.

| Variant | Where OD enters | Non-dominant-eye monocular CRF | OD index vs contrast | Effect of the other eye on a monocular unit |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| (i) | Excitatory numerator only; balanced pool | Rmax-scaled | Constant | Suppressed |
| (ii) | Numerator and pool | c50-shifted | Shrinks toward zero as both eyes saturate | Untouched |
| (iii) Georgeson–Sengpiel | Early OD-independent suppression, then late OD-weighted compensatory gain control | — | — | — |

Four findings fit variant (i), in which **OD describes the numerator, not the pool**: Mitchell 2023's Rmax-only result, Zhang 2024's suppression of eye-preferring neurons, Hallum 2017's balanced suppression, and Dougherty 2019's suppression of monocular neurons. That reading lets both camps be right. A "monocular" neuron still receives suppressive input from its silent eye, which is the subthreshold binocularity Freeman and Ohzawa described. It remains an interpretation, because no study has fitted excitatory and pool weights separately in isolated units across a dichoptic contrast matrix, and multiunit signals mix neurons of different OD.

The saturation ratio (1 + a_N/a_D)/(1 + w) supplies a direct test (derived for this report). At high contrast, binocular modulation should change sign where the OD ratio a_N/a_D equals w, so the OD at which modulation crosses zero is itself an estimate of w. On that logic, Zhang et al.'s enhancement of balanced neurons at 0.9 contrast implies w < 1 for those neurons, provided they were near saturation. Cox et al. found that stronger ocular bias predicts more sustained suppression (r = −0.40, congruent stimuli), which is the same relation seen from the other side ([Cox et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6797477)).

The direct conflict between electrophysiology and imaging remains open. Zhang et al. describe Dougherty 2019 as finding "only monocular neurons, but not binocular neurons, being significantly suppressed by binocular stimulation," whereas they found binocular neurons enhanced. They attribute the gap to temporal resolution: spike windows of 50–200 ms versus calcium signals integrated over up to 1000 ms ([Zhang et al. 2024](https://doi.org/10.7554/elife.92839)).

### Laminar evidence is suggestive but rests on a handful of neurons per layer

The classic starting point holds that "in layer IV most cells are driven by one eye only," while layers II–III and V–VI are mostly binocular ([Hubel & Wiesel 1968 · mac, lightly anesthetized](https://doi.org/10.1113/jphysiol.1968.sp008455)). Awake laminar work has eroded that picture. Dougherty et al. found that 22% of 290 single units were monocular, and **11 of 17 monocular L4 neurons were significantly modulated by binocular stimulation**, mostly by suppression ([Dougherty et al. 2019 · mac-W](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2018.12.004)). Seven of the 10 facilitated monocular neurons were in L4, where facilitation "tended to occur at the onset of the visual response," while suppression came "several milliseconds" later. Median modulation onset (62 ms) trailed median response onset (50 ms).

Cox et al. split the response into two windows ([Cox et al. 2019 · mac-W](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6797477)). In the transient (50–100 ms), single-unit responses exceeded monocular responses for both congruent (+13.73 spikes/s) and incongruent (+5.85) stimuli. In the sustained window (150–250 ms), congruent facilitation vanished while incongruent stimuli were suppressed (−5.57 spikes/s). At 50–100 ms the granular current sink was larger than monocular by about 480–495 nA/mm² for both binocular conditions. It later returned to the monocular level for congruent stimuli. Their laminar breakdown rests on **4–7 equiocular units per layer**.

Two results from other preparations bear on laminar location. In cat, "response-gain control prevailed in layer 4, whereas cells in layers 2/3, 5 and 6 mainly showed contrast-gain control" ([Sengpiel et al. 1998 · cat-A](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0042-6989(97)00413-6)). In macaque, repetition adaptation transfers across the eyes and is localized mainly to supragranular processing ([Westerberg et al. 2019 · mac-W](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6589708)).

The laminar latency sequence is consistent across methods. Layers 4C and 6 respond first, and the most superficial neurons lag 4C by about 15 ms ([Maunsell & Gibson 1992 · mac](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1432087/)). Measured from the earliest (L4C) sink, CSD sinks begin about 23 ms later in L2/3 and 61 ms later in deep-lower layers ([Bijanzadeh et al. 2018 · mac-A](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10110109)). The differences that discriminate mechanisms span **5–60 ms**: fast vs slow suppression, L4C vs L2/3, and near vs far surround. The legacy pipeline's 3.4–12 ms onset error and ±3.4 ms spike-time error were as large as Tanabe and Cumming's 7 ms delay and Dougherty's ~12 ms modulation lag. Photodiode timing at about 1 ms on the corrected clock is now adequate.

Human and cross-species laminar data cannot settle the locus. 7T laminar and columnar fMRI attributes eye-specific rivalry to "interocular inhibition between adjacent ocular dominance columns in the superficial layers," not to the eye-specific layers of the LGN ([Qian et al. 2025 · human](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-025-02320-4)). But gradient-echo BOLD eye selectivity peaks superficially for vascular reasons ([Zaretskaya et al. 2020 · human](https://doaj.org/article/0d45249dd3de444aa9970c8f6cae2073)). Only CBV-weighted fMRI peaks at mid-depth for eye-of-origin decoding ([7T CBV vs BOLD decoding · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12407697)).

In amblyopia, monocular activity is attenuated and delayed in the thalamic-input layers, followed by "imbalanced binocular suppression and weakened binocular integration in the superficial layers" ([Wang et al. 2025 · human](https://doi.org/10.1162/imag_a_00561)). Binocular integration is widespread across layers in mouse ([Fu, Tanabe & Cang 2023 · mouse](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.0925-23.2023)). A 4Cα network model predicts binocular strips at the borders of ocular-dominance columns, with largely monocular L6 feedback ([Xiao, Lin & Young 2026 · model, preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.21785)).

Layer assignment is itself contested. An early CSD sink was identifiable in only **51% of 810 probes**, and the spectrolaminar alpha/beta–gamma crossover in 61–81% depending on method ([Mendoza-Halliday et al. 2024 · mac, multi-lab](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10917659)). V1 is an exception there, because its deep alpha/beta power is likely volume-conducted from V2. A critique found that the crossover identified L4 in only **29–33%** of recordings ([Mackey et al. 2024 · preprint](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.09.18.613490v4)). The spike–LFP phase-reversal marker was validated in V4, MT and PFC but never in V1 ([Davis et al. 2023 · macaque V4, marmoset](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10162800)). These limits bear directly on a dataset whose layer labels are uncertain by ±0.5–1 mm. The notes found no primate study of any of the following: monocular-versus-dioptic CRF parameters by layer, onset latency of interocular suppression by layer, laminar CSD at high versus low contrast, 4Cα and 4Cβ separated for binocular modulation, or L5 and L6 binocular modulation reported separately.

### Models disagree most on the equivalent-contrast factor and the dichoptic dip

Most binocular normalization models belong to one family. Eye-specific excitation raised to an exponent is divided by a pool that contains both eyes, with interocular weight w. The models differ on three choices: whether the exponent is applied before or after the eyes are summed, the value of w, and whether a second gain stage follows the sum.

| Model | Equation (L, R = eye contrasts) | Exponent placement | w | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Monocular normalization + linear sum | r(L) + r(R), r = a·cⁿ/(cⁿ+σⁿ) | before | 0 | [Moradi & Heeger 2009 Eq. 3](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747122/) |
| Binocular normalization = late summation | (Lᵖ + Rᵖ)/(Zᵠ + Lᵠ + Rᵠ) | before | 1 | [MH 2009 Eq. 6](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747122/); [Baker & Wade 2017 Eq. 4](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5903417/) |
| Early summation | (L+R)ᵖ/(Zᵠ + (L+R)ᵠ) | after | implicit 1 | [Baker & Wade 2017 Eq. 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5903417/) |
| Orientation-weighted normalization | Lⁿ/(Lⁿ + wRⁿ + σⁿ) + Rⁿ/(Rⁿ + wLⁿ + σⁿ) | before | free (1 same-ori; 0.52 orthogonal) | [MH 2009 Eq. 10](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747122/) |
| Two-stage | Stage 1 Lᵐ/(S+L+R) + Rᵐ/(S+R+L); stage 2 Bᵖ/(Z+Bᵠ) | m ≈ 1.2–1.5 before | 1 | [Meese et al. 2006](https://doi.org/10.1167/6.11.7); [Baker, Meese & Georgeson 2007](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/1426/1/AURA1426.pdf) |
| Gain control of gain control | each eye attenuates the other and the other's gain control, ∝ contrast energy | — | — | [Ding & Sperling 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1347993/) |
| Ocular opponency | rectified L − R channels subtractively inhibit monocular units | — | — | [Said & Heeger 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3610603/) |

Moradi–Heeger Eq. 6 (fMRI) and Baker–Wade Eq. 4 (SSVEP) are algebraically identical when p = q. Two groups using different methods therefore converged on the same answer: exponent before summation, binocular pool, w ≈ 1. Above threshold, the two-stage model acts as a weighted average. Its stage 1 can be rewritten as binsum = w_L·L^(m−1) + w_R·R^(m−1) with w_L ≈ L/(L+R), which is "Hering's law of complementary shares" at suprathreshold contrast ([Baker, Meese & Georgeson 2007](https://publications.aston.ac.uk/id/eprint/1426/1/AURA1426.pdf)). The Ding–Sperling model is linear "at very low contrast" and becomes "Contrast-Weighted Summation" at high contrast. Its gain-control filter has a time constant of about 110 ms, and combination at 50 ms exposures "is well approximated by... linear addition" ([Ding & Sperling 2006 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1347993/)).

Where the exponent sits fixes the dioptic-versus-monocular relation, whatever the parameter values (derived). With the exponent before summation and w = 1, R(c,c) = R_mono(2^(1/n)·c). With linear summation before any monotone nonlinearity, R(c,c) = R_mono(2c). With w = 0, R(c,c) = 2·R_mono(c), which exceeds the monocular ceiling. In the two-stage and Ding–Sperling models, the factor falls toward 1 as contrast rises.

The team's grid is nearly octave-spaced (5.5, 11, 22.5, 45, 90%), so (c,c) and (2c,0) are both measured directly. That allows a test of "linear summation, then any nonlinearity" that needs no fitted parameters and does not depend on the output nonlinearity. It does assume equal eye weights, and 22.5 is 2.3% above 2 × 11.

The non-monotone dip is a neural analogue of Fechner's paradox. In stage 1 of the two-stage model, the derivative with respect to the weak eye at zero contrast is −R^m/(S+R)², which is negative (derived). The dip is therefore a sign test that does not depend on the output nonlinearity.

Dynamics are where the static models fall silent. Interocular suppression weight grows with stimulus duration ([Baker, Meese & Summers 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2007.01.030)), and Ding–Sperling gain control integrates over about 110 ms. The notes infer that recurrent normalization should give more linear combination early and more normalization late; this covers Said–Heeger (τ = 50 ms) and stabilized supralinear networks (SSN). No peer-reviewed binocular SSN exists. The only one found is a master's thesis known from a search summary ([Selby 2016](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/10264)), so SSN predictions for dichoptic surfaces are extrapolations.

A 6×6 matrix can identify several things (derived): each eye's CRF, w in each direction, exponent placement (through k), the dip, contour geometry (linear, superellipse, ratio or difference), and whether OD enters the numerator or the pool. It cannot separate second-stage parameters from a unit's own output nonlinearity. Truchard et al. hit the same wall: they could not separate the binocular gain site from the output exponent. With only five nonzero levels per eye, the exponents p and q are also only weakly identified separately.

### Human data demand a binocular pool but cannot say where it sits

Population signals in human V1 consistently favour interocular normalization. In fMRI, adding a matched grating to the second eye at 10% contrast gave an **additivity index of 0.01** (95% CI −0.23 to 0.25), where a hemodynamic power law alone predicts 0.46–0.57 ([Moradi & Heeger 2009 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2747122/)). Monocular normalization followed by linear summation failed. Eye-tagged SSVEP favoured late summation with p = q = 2.22 and Z = 9.48%, explaining 92% of variance and 73% of held-out masking conditions ([Baker & Wade 2017 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5903417/)).

Source-imaged SSVEP found one divisive gain control shared by self-terms and intermodulation terms. Interocular suppression was about 50% in V1 and V3a and about 20% in LOC ([Hou, Nicholas & Verghese 2020 · human](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.2043-19.2020)). A follow-up found excitatory and suppressive exponents near 2.4 and a target weight near 1.45 ([Hou et al. 2021 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8513700)). In MEG, V1 dichoptic masking fit a normalization model ([Chadnova et al. 2018 · human](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.10.035)). A neutral-density filter that delayed one eye by about 38 ms weakened that eye's masking, which required a temporal filter before combination.

The type of gain change is unsettled. A Bayesian meta-analysis of 16 SSVEP studies favoured contrast gain, but with overlapping credible intervals ([Baker, Vilidaite & Wade 2021 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8544832/)). New 5 Hz data in the same paper showed contrast gain for monocular and dichoptic masks at the first harmonic and a mix at the second. The 2017 eye experiment, by contrast, looked response-gain-like. Suppression as a proportion of the monocular response is constant across luminance and cone-opponent gratings, and significant first-order intermodulation terms place combination in neurons carrying half-wave-rectified signals ([Carter et al. 2025 · human](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12007675)).

Amblyopia dissociates the two components. Binocular summation "remains intact" once sensitivities are equated ([Baker et al. 2007 · human](https://eprints.soton.ac.uk/49281)). In strabismic amblyopes the excitatory exponent drops to 0.8 (controls 2.4) while the suppressive exponent is unchanged ([Hou et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8513700)). Two hours of patching boosts the deprived eye's 7T V1 BOLD response, and the boost correlates across people with that eye's gain in rivalry dominance ([Binda et al. 2018 · human](https://elifesciences.org/articles/40014v1)).

Human researchers explicitly defer the implementation question to neurophysiology. Moradi and Heeger noted that their result "might seem at odds with single cell recordings that suggest contrast normalization and cross-orientation suppression are mainly monocular." Baker and Wade framed their model as algorithm-level and expect "single-cell neurophysiology" to reveal how it is implemented. The notes found **no human laminar study of binocular contrast combination across contrast levels**; all laminar human work concerns eye-of-origin or rivalry.

The human data therefore set four benchmarks that a V1 population read-out has to meet: a threshold binocular advantage of about 1.5–1.7, near-zero advantage above threshold, dichoptic masking stronger than monoptic masking, and w ≈ 1. They also suggest a reconciliation with the cat data. Truchard's mostly monocular gain control in cat simple cells and the human w ≈ 1 are compatible if interocular normalization strengthens beyond the input stage and over the response, and if pooled human signals over-weight superficial output layers. **That hypothesis is testable here as a depth-and-epoch gradient in w and k.** According to a search summary, Mitchell's 2024 dissertation proposes a version of it: a normalization whose strength grows through the microcircuit ([Mitchell 2024](https://ir.vanderbilt.edu/handle/1803/19176)).

## Sixteen open questions, ranked by what only this dataset adds

Rank combines three criteria. The first is leverage: whether the answer adjudicates a live controversy between named models. The second is novelty: whether it needs something Mitchell 2022/2023 lacked, such as depth resolution, epochs, isolated units, two probes, LFP/CSD or corrected timing. The third is statistical power.

The verdicts rest on the dataset's actual constraints. Depth below the top of cortex is the robust axis across 26 penetrations, while named layers rest on 22 L4/5 landmarks of modest confidence (±0.5–1 mm). Analog MUA (581 driven channels) is the workhorse, because isolated units are scarce: 68 strict or 136 lenient, with only about 15–50 driven per condition. Each cell has about 30 presentations, which the notes translate into a condition-difference standard error of roughly **6.5% per unit** (assuming ~20 spikes per window and a Fano factor of 1–1.5). The transient epoch runs about 40–150 ms and the sustained epoch 150–317 ms (≤217 ms in session 211008). Gratings are static, identical in the two eyes and at zero disparity, and only one eye was tracked except on some B days.

### Running the top four as one pipeline

Questions 1–4 share inputs: channel-wise, trial-averaged aMUA and CSD for all 36 conditions on the corrected clock. They should run as one pipeline.

Align epochs to each channel's own response onset rather than to fixed post-stimulus windows. Laminar onsets differ by 15–25 ms ([Maunsell & Gibson 1992](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1432087/); [Bijanzadeh et al. 2018](https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10110109)), so a fixed 50–150 ms window would compare different phases of the response in granular and superficial channels. A short onset epoch, the first ~25 ms after each channel's onset, is worth adding. Ding–Sperling predicts linear combination for brief exposures, and Dougherty's L4 facilitation falls in that window.

k and w carry complementary information. k tests exponent placement without fitted parameters, while w (MH Eq. 10 with eye-specific parameters) tests pool composition. Both need stratification by OD. MUA dilutes OD, which helps the k test because balanced channels are common, but hurts question 5, which needs isolated units.

The latency analysis should report facilitation and suppression onsets separately, along with how each depends on non-dominant-eye contrast. An LGN-inherited signal predicts suppression locked to the channel's response onset at every N contrast. A cortically driven pool instead predicts a lag after response onset that shortens as N contrast rises. Both predictions are derived.

### Controls that every analysis needs

The stimuli are static, phase-0, identical gratings at zero disparity, seen through a mirror stereoscope, and that design carries three specific confounds. First, in units whose preferred disparity is not zero, diagonal cells mix interocular normalization with disparity tuning (the energy-model cross-term). Second, trial-to-trial vergence error converts into interocular phase noise. A 0.1° error at 2 c/deg shifts interocular phase by 72° (derived), which phase-specific, simple-like cells turn into variable facilitation or suppression ([Ohzawa & Freeman 1986a](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1986.56.1.221); [Smith et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1997.78.1.351)). Third, vergence can be checked only on the B days with second-eye data. Those days should be used to estimate how much binocular-modulation variance vergence explains, and key results should be reported by ocularity class.

Across all analyses, fit models by cross-validated likelihood, hierarchically across channels with depth as a covariate. Pre-register the nested comparisons: the SSVEP meta-analysis showed that per-study tests of gain mode are routinely inconclusive ([Baker, Vilidaite & Wade 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8544832/)).

## What a static, iso-oriented, zero-disparity matrix cannot decide

Because orientation is identical in the two eyes, the design cannot address dichoptic cross-orientation suppression or the congruent-versus-incongruent comparison. That includes the deep-layer early suppression that Cox et al. found only for incongruent stimuli ([Cox et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6797477)). It also rules out Moradi and Heeger's same-orientation versus orthogonal weights (1 vs 0.52), conflict-gated suppression ([Katyal et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5775389)) and rivalry onset ([Carlson et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.isci.2023.107359)). Contrast imbalance does not substitute for orientation conflict. Without monoptic plaids or masks, the design cannot separate the ipsiocular suppression route from the interocular one ([Baker, Meese & Summers 2007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroscience.2007.01.030); [Li et al. 2005](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.00203.2005)).

The static gratings exclude three temporal tests: the temporal-frequency cutoffs that diagnose cortical interocular suppression ([Sengpiel & Vorobyov 2005](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.0862-05.2005)), the speed invariance of dichoptic masking ([Meese & Baker 2009](https://doi.org/10.1167/9.5.2)), and F1/F0 simple/complex classification. Zero disparity and phase 0 exclude the energy-model cross-term ([Ohzawa, DeAngelis & Freeman 1990](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.2396096)). They also exclude the phase predictions that separate Ding–Sperling from the two-stage model ([Ding & Sperling 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1347993/)), and any test of whether combination precedes rectification ([Smith et al. 1997](https://doi.org/10.1152/jn.1997.78.1.366); [Truchard et al. 2000](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6772209/)).

Simultaneous onset with ~317 ms gaps rules out Sengpiel's "already responding" sequence dependence ([Sengpiel et al. 1994](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.14-11-06855.1994)) and staggered or flash-suppression designs ([Economides et al. 2021](https://doi.org/10.1523/jneurosci.0044-21.2021)). It also rules out interocular delay manipulations ([Chadnova et al. 2018](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuroimage.2017.10.035)) and frequency-tagged intermodulation spectra; for the latter, the static interaction contrast is the substitute. Without pharmacology or optogenetics, GABAergic mediation and SSN regimes cannot be tested directly, so feedforward and recurrent normalization can be told apart only through timing and laminar pattern.

Resolution and regime limits close off several more questions. 4Cα versus 4Cβ is out, given 100 µm spacing plus ±0.5–1 mm label uncertainty. So is separating second-stage gain control from the output nonlinearity, and so is the threshold regime below 5.5%, where the two-stage model predicts that monocular and binocular responses differ most. LGN origin cannot be tested beyond an indirect corner test, responses beyond ~317 ms are not recorded, and two sampling points cannot define a two-dimensional travelling wave. Supersaturation is settled for this dataset and needs no further analysis.

## Conclusion

The field's disagreements are not contradictions. They line up along three axes: measurement scale (simple cells versus BOLD), response epoch (onset transient versus sustained response) and circuit depth (input versus output layers). That makes the reconciliation hypothesis concrete and falsifiable here. If interocular normalization is weak in the earliest granular response and strengthens superficially and over time, three things should follow. k should fall toward 1, w should rise with distance from the input sink and between epochs, and suppression onset should lag excitation by more than ~10 ms outside the input band. A flat depth profile would instead support the psychophysical claim that interocular suppression already acts at a pre-summation stage everywhere. It would also make the cat "monocular-first" result a species or preparation difference.

Two derivations raise the stakes beyond what Mitchell et al. 2023 could test. First, single-stage interocular normalization predicts pure contrast-gain shifts for other-eye suppression of a strictly monocular unit and for the dioptic CRF of a balanced unit. Any Rmax change must then come from eye-weight imbalance relative to w. Response-gain suppression in strictly monocular units would therefore point to a separate, later gain stage, as the cat result already hints. Second, the best-supported reading of current data is that ocular dominance sets the numerator while the pool stays eye-balanced, and only isolated units can test that.

Latency is now the dataset's most distinctive asset, because the corrected clock removed an error as large as the effects being sought. The efficient order is to settle the laminar markers first, run questions 1 and 2 together from shared PSTHs, then questions 3 and 4, and spend single-unit power on question 5.
