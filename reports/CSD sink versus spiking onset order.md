# Input-layer MUA barely outruns the sink

No macaque V1 study has measured the order of the input-layer (4C) current sink and multiunit spiking with one onset estimator applied to both signals on the same contacts. The papers that address it say the two **coincide** ([van Kerkoerle et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4210002/); [Gieselmann & Thiele 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8916775/)), or that the **sink comes slightly first** for single units, with no millisecond values given ([Westerberg et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6589708/)). The only published "spikes first" number, 23 ms for spikes against 39 ms for the CSD, was dismissed by its own authors as a difference of methods and stimuli ([Bijanzadeh et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6279245/)). Single-axon recordings in rabbit and cat set the physiological order precisely. The thalamocortical terminal fires first, the monosynaptic sink begins **about 0.7 ms later**, and cortical spikes follow once enough input has converged ([Swadlow et al. 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6757983); [Jin et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556869)). Analog MUA can therefore lead the sink without breaking causality, because thalamic afferent spikes can be recorded in layer 4. At onset, though, that lead should be about a millisecond. Our own registered measurement (project data, not literature) puts MUA half-max **2.2–2.5 ms** ahead of the sink current after correcting for the 0.9 ms LFP filter delay, which exceeds that window. When the current comes from the single sink contact with no smoothing, the lead in the two registered conditions falls to **0.5 and 1.4 ms**, and only 13 and 17 of 26 penetrations agree in sign. The simplest reading is that the three-contact, Hamming-smoothed current borrows later currents from neighbouring depths, and that the remaining sub-millisecond-to-1 ms lead matches afferent spikes in the MUA. Which signal peaks first is unresolved. The paper can therefore say that spiking at the sink depth does not lag the sink current. It cannot say that spiking precedes synaptic input, and the sink cannot serve as a time reference finer than a few milliseconds.

## Macaque studies report coincidence, never a measured lead

The Schroeder lineage established the 4C sink as the cortical marker of geniculate input, but it applied its onset rule to the CSD alone. Schroeder, Mehta & Givre measured onset once per penetration on AVREC, the rectified CSD averaged across all channels. Onset was the first point more than 2 SD above baseline that stayed there for 8 of the next 10 samples. They report **4C onsets of 26–29 ms** to diffuse flashes. They chose CSD over MUA as "a more direct index of afferent input" and describe the sinks and "the associated increase in multiunit activity" as co-occurring, without stating an order ([Schroeder et al. 1998](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~msereno/569/readings/CSD-visualareas.pdf)). That paper also contains the mechanistic hint that matters here. Active white matter shows "multiunit activity in the presence of a near-zero second derivative", so MUA can register afferent axons that leave no local CSD ([Schroeder et al. 1998](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~msereno/569/readings/CSD-visualareas.pdf)). Givre and colleagues' wavelength study implies a 4C current-flow onset of about 23 ms, but only by subtracting 24 ms from a 47 ms effect latency; it gives no MUA onset ([Givre et al. 1995](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/visual-neuroscience/article/effects-of-wavelength-on-the-timing-and-laminar-distribution-of-illuminanceevoked-activity-in-macaque-v1/A6F43C4DE063E98F41A3A3526EF1849D)).

The later laminar work uses the sink and the MUA together as markers of layer 4 and rarely compares their timing. Ferro and colleagues identify layer IV by "the earliest current sink of the CSD and the shortest multiunit response latency" ([Ferro et al. 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8000025/)). The three explicit statements disagree in direction and come from different spike signals, which may be the most informative fact in this literature.

The two analog-MUA studies report coincidence, and the one single-unit study reports a sink lead. That split is what one would expect if the earliest analog MUA in 4C includes presynaptic afferent spikes and sorted units are mostly postsynaptic cortical cells. No primate study tested this. The Bijanzadeh gap is not evidence of anything: CSD onset there was a 3–5 SD criterion on a flashed square, while spiking onset came from gratings with a different method. The authors say explicitly that the 16 ms difference reflects "the different methods we used" ([Bijanzadeh et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6279245/)). Primate auditory cortex points the same way as the afferent idea. A CSD methods review summarizing the Steinschneider–Schroeder A1 recordings attributes the initial cortical negativity to "the thalamocortical afferent volley, immediately followed by postsynaptic activation" ([Tenke & Kayser 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3498576/)). That source quantifies no MUA-versus-sink interval.

## Thalamic terminals fire about 0.7 ms before their sink

Spike-triggered CSD from single thalamocortical neurons gives the cleanest timing anywhere in this literature. In awake rabbit S1, each thalamic spike produces a brief biphasic or triphasic **axon-terminal potential in layer 4 at about 0.8 ms**, followed by a monosynaptic sink. The sink starts 1.2–1.8 ms after the thalamic spike, and the mean interval from terminal potential to sink onset is **0.72 ms** ([Swadlow et al. 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6757983)). In rabbit V1 the sink follows the axonal potential by under 1 ms and rises 10–90% in about 0.45 ms. The two components also separate by history: the terminal potential is unaffected by the preceding interspike interval, while the postsynaptic sink depresses ([Stoelzel et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713607/)). Cat V1 shows the same structure. A fast axonal component appears 0.8–2.1 ms after the geniculate spike and matches the spike recorded from the geniculate terminal, and the postsynaptic sink follows after a synaptic delay of about 0.5 ms. After muscimol silenced the cortex, the authors recorded **182 geniculate afferents as units throughout layer 4** ([Jin et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556869)). Afferent spikes are therefore large enough to appear in a high-pass MUA band at the input layer. In rat S1 the earliest cortical response to thalamic stimulation (1.0–1.6 ms) was unrelated to cortical unit firing and was attributed to synchronous thalamocortical fibres ([Kandel & Buzsáki 1997](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6573130)).

Field theory explains why the sink reflects synapses and not spikes, and why that fixes the order for postsynaptic cells. Mitzdorf's review concluded that CSDs are essentially EPSP currents. Fibres of passage spread their current over 1–2 mm and cannot be resolved, and somatic spikes cancel almost completely once their latency scatter reaches about 1.5 ms. Terminal arborizations, by contrast, produce a localized, monophasic sink/source pair ([Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)). Mitzdorf also notes that the capacitive current tracks the slope of the postsynaptic potential, with no net current during its decay ([Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)). The sink current therefore peaks before the membrane potential it drives, and spikes of the postsynaptic cells should follow it. A biophysical model of the thalamocortical response adds one refinement: fast-spiking interneurons carry the earliest part of the monosynaptic CSD (minimum 1.89 ms) and regular-spiking cells the later part (3.10 ms) ([Hagen et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5444196/)).

The population sink is not the monosynaptic sink scaled up, and this changes what half-max and peak comparisons measure. Muscimol silencing of gerbil A1 left the granular sink's onset unchanged (p = 0.92) but cut its peak by **about 88%** ([Happel et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6633479/)). In rat S1, thalamocortical synapses make up only about 20% of layer 4 synapses, and "the major current flow derives from the activity of the intracortical circuitry" ([Kandel & Buzsáki 1997](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6573130)). Fitted rat barrel models give feedforward thalamic time constants of 3–6 ms against 8–10 ms for recurrent excitation ([Blomquist et al. 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2653726/)). If V1 works the same way, the sink's half-max and peak fall in a recurrent phase driven by the cortical spikes that precede it. A spikes-first result at half-max is then the expected order, not a violation of it. The species transfer is uncertain: a model fitted to mouse V1 flash responses assigns the early L4 sink "primarily" to thalamocortical synapses ([Rimehaug et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.87169)). In rodents the earliest sinks and spikes can also sit at the L5/6 border rather than in L4 ([Sakata & Harris 2009](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2778614); [Constantinople & Bruno 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4203320)). In macaque V1 the lower deep layers lag 4C by tens of milliseconds ([Bijanzadeh et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6279245/)), so the earliest sink in our probes is safely 4C. No study in any species reports the share of intact-cortex layer 4 MUA that comes from thalamic afferents, so the size of the afferent lead in a population signal is unmeasured.

## Spatial blending is the processing step big enough to matter

Most processing choices shift a CSD-versus-MUA comparison by less than a millisecond at half-max. The exception is spatial blending of the sink current, which shifts it by more than the physiology does. The ERP literature shows why filter causality matters for onsets. Zero-phase low-pass filters below about 100 Hz smear an event backward in time and make onsets look early, while peaks are mostly spared ([VanRullen 2011](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2011.00365/full)). Causal filters instead delay onsets by roughly their group delay ([Rousselet 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00131/full); [Widmann & Schröger 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00233/full)). Our LFP chain ends in the amplifier's causal fourth-order 500 Hz low-pass, whose DC group delay is 2.613/(2π·500) = **0.83 ms**. That agrees with the project's measured 0.9 ms (project QC, [docs/09 check J](../docs/09_paper_design.md)). The project's high-pass is a causal 0.3 Hz first-order stage, so it avoids the large acausal high-pass artefacts reported in the ERP work ([Rousselet 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00131/full)). Simulations run for this review (`research_notes/CSD sink versus spiking onset order/sim/`: `latbias.py`, `latbias2.py`, `depthmix_thr.py`) put three zero-phase steps at about zero bias at half-max: the 200 Hz envelope low-pass, the 500–5000 Hz band-pass and the σ = 2 ms Gaussian. A causal 200 Hz envelope filter would have delayed the MUA by about 2.3 ms, so the zero-phase choice matters. The Gaussian is the strongest temporal smoother in either chain (−3 dB at about 66 Hz). It shifts 10% crossings 1–2 ms earlier and peaks 1.5–3.3 ms later, depending on how sharp the transient is. Change-point onsets and peak comparisons should therefore be made without it.

Onset criteria can produce a lead from nothing. Half-max of each signal's own peak is immune to scale and baseline offset ([Lee, Williford & Maunsell 2007](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6672969)), but it confounds onset with rise time. In simulation, two signals with identical onsets whose full rises differ by 6 ms differ by 3 ms at half-max. That difference shrinks to about 1.8 ms at the 10% crossing and grows at the peak. The registered lead does not have this signature: it is 3.8, 3.5, 3.3 and 3.5 ms at 10, 25, 50 and 75% (project, [docs/09 check J](../docs/09_paper_design.md)), which rules out a large rise-time difference as the main cause. Mixed criteria and fixed-SD thresholds bias the comparison in more familiar ways. The legacy `dichoptic_csd.py` compared MUA at 50% with the sink at 25%, which favoured the sink. A k·SD rule favours whichever signal has the higher signal-to-noise ratio. Published criteria range from 2 SD on rectified, channel-averaged CSD ([Schroeder et al. 1998](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~msereno/569/readings/CSD-visualareas.pdf)) to 3–5 SD on per-depth CSD ([Bijanzadeh et al. 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6279245/)). Model-based estimators, which perform best by mean squared error ([Friedman & Priebe 1998](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9765132)), separate departure from baseline from the mid-rise. They are also the estimators most sensitive to backward smearing from zero-phase smoothing.

Spatial operations add no temporal delay, but they mix depths, and our sink current mixes more than the earlier review notes assumed. The three-point Hamming kernel actually used (0.23/0.54/0.23), convolved with the three-contact mean, gives effective weights on CSD contacts of **0.077, 0.257, 0.333, 0.257, 0.077** (our arithmetic from the stated weights). The sink contact therefore supplies only a third of the "sink current", and 15% comes from contacts 200 µm away. Through the second difference, the current depends on LFP contacts within ±300 µm. At Neuropixels resolution, the short-latency sink in macaque V1 spans only about 200 µm, narrower than 4C ([Zhang, Li & Callaway 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10849622/)). The MUA in the same comparison is an equal-weight mean over three contacts and is the most local of the three signals: MUA listens over a smaller area than CSD, and CSD over a smaller area than LFP ([Kajikawa & Schroeder 2011](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240862)). The two signals in check J therefore have footprints of roughly 500 µm and 300 µm. In simulation, if each 100 µm step away from the sink delays activation by 1–2 ms, the blended current reaches half-max 0.5–1.5 ms late, with a nearly flat 10–75% profile. Steeper gradients of 4 ms per step give 1.6–2.9 ms, with a lead that grows toward the peak. A flat threshold profile therefore does not separate a true rigid lead from blending across a shallow onset gradient. It only rules out steep gradients and large rise-time differences as the sole cause. Standard versus inverse CSD changes timing only through the same depth mixing ([Pettersen et al. 2006](https://nmr.mgh.harvard.edu/publications/journal_articles/3942)); no paper quantifies its effect on sink onset.

## Our 2.5 ms lead shrinks toward the afferent window

The following are project measurements, not literature (all from [docs/09 check J](../docs/09_paper_design.md)). There are 26 laminar penetrations with 100 µm contacts. Analog MUA is computed zero-phase from the 30 kHz stream. LFP is the amplifier's causally filtered 1 kHz stream, read on the same NSP tick clock, and its only offset from the MUA is the 0.9 ms filter delay. The registered tests asked whether the sink current at the earliest grating-evoked sink starts (J1) and peaks (J2) before the MUA on the same contacts, using identical estimators. **Both failed, and the estimate pointed the other way.** MUA half-max preceded the current's by 3.3 ms for the dominant eye at 90% contrast, in 22/26 penetrations (two-sided sign test p = .0005, our calculation), and by 3.0 ms for both eyes at 45%. With the simulated filter bias removed, the leads are 2.5 ms (95% CI −5.1 to −0.5) and 2.2 ms (−3.7 to −0.3). The peak lead of 3.5 ms has a CI of −6 to +1 ms, which includes zero. A descriptive sensitivity analysis corrected the current for the 0.9 ms delay and removed the three-contact mean, the Hamming kernel and the Gaussian. The half-max lead then fell to **0.5 ms (13/26 MUA earlier, sign test p = 1.0)** for the dominant eye at 90% and **1.4 ms (17/26, p = .17)** for both eyes at 45%. Both eyes at 90% kept **2.6 ms (16/26, p = .33)**. Across all processing variants, the half-max lead ranged from 0.5 to 2.7 ms, and peak differences ranged from 5 ms earlier to 1 ms later.

Read against the literature, these numbers support one account more than the others. The registered 2.2–2.5 ms lead, and especially the roughly 3 ms lead at the 10% crossing, is larger than the 0.5–0.7 ms separating a thalamocortical terminal spike from its sink ([Swadlow et al. 2002](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6757983); [Stoelzel et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713607/)). It is about the size that blending a five-contact current over a shallow onset gradient produces, and it shrinks by 0.8–2 ms when the spatial averaging and smoothing are removed. The residual 0.5–1.4 ms in the two registered conditions lies inside the afferent window. The recurrent build-up account, in which sink amplitude is mostly intracortical current driven by earlier cortical spikes ([Happel et al. 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6633479/)), explains why half-max can favour spikes. It predicts a lead that grows toward the peak, however, and the registered threshold profile is flat. Envelope compression can contribute up to about 1 ms. The single-contact variant is not automatically the unbiased one. If the sink contact is chosen as the earliest sink in the same trials, its current tends to be early by chance, which shrinks the lead. Noise in an unsmoothed trace can push the half-max crossing either way, depending on whether the estimator searches forward from baseline or backward from the peak. The best current bracket is a true half-max lead of roughly **0.5–2.5 ms**, with the sign holding on average in every variant but not penetration by penetration in the cleanest one.

The condition dependence is itself a warning. The dominant-eye 90% condition was the most consistent under registered processing (22/26) and the least consistent under the cleanest processing (13/26). Both eyes at 90% kept a 2.6 ms mean on only 16/26 penetrations, which suggests a few large leads drive that mean. Until medians, sign counts and a factorial breakdown are reported, no difference between conditions is interpretable.

The comparison with Westerberg et al. resolves into a testable prediction rather than a conflict. That study found sink onset before spiking for single units in the same species and laboratory lineage ([Westerberg et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6589708/)). Our study finds analog MUA at or before the sink. Single units in intact cortex are overwhelmingly cortical cells; Jin and colleagues had to silence the cortex to isolate geniculate afferents as units ([Jin et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556869)). Both results fit an order of afferent spikes, then sink, then cortical spikes. Under that order, analog MUA leads or coincides with the sink and single units lag it.

For the paper, this supports the project's existing rule that "earliest sink" marks the input depth and is not a time reference. One wording the data can carry: at the depth of the earliest sink, multiunit activity reached half its peak 0.5–2.7 ms before the sink current, depending on spatial smoothing. The methods should state both filter chains and the 0.9 ms LFP delay. Analysis windows of 10 ms or more are essentially unaffected by this ambiguity. A millisecond-scale latency claim, such as a laminar onset order or an interocular latency difference, should compare one signal with itself across conditions, as [Gieselmann & Thiele 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8916775/) did with MUAe across depth. It should never compare the sink with spikes. Legacy CSD-versus-MUA latencies carry the 3.4 ms clock artefact and the mixed 50%/25% criteria, so they are void.

## Three checks on existing data would settle sign and size

**Sorted units are the most decisive test, because they separate presynaptic from postsynaptic spiking without new recordings.** Take the Kilosort/phy units on the sink contacts and measure their half-max and low-threshold onsets against the same sink current, with the same estimator. The afferent account predicts that units coincide with or lag the sink while analog MUA on the same contacts leads it, which would reproduce the Westerberg et al. direction within our own data. If units also lead by 2 ms, spatial blending or recurrent build-up must carry the effect.

**The processing analysis needs three changes before any number goes in the paper.** First, choose the sink contact on odd presentations and measure on even ones, so the cleanest variant is free of selection bias. Second, report the factorial grid (contacts × Hamming × Gaussian, plus spline inverse CSD) as medians with sign counts. Third, match the temporal chains directly: run the MUA through the amplifier's causal 500 Hz response instead of subtracting a simulated bias, confirm that MUA downsampling is zero-phase, and add change-point onsets on unsmoothed data. A causal chain avoids the backward smearing that makes zero-phase smoothing distort onsets ([Widmann & Schröger 2012](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2012.00233/full)).

**The repetition structure of bminteroc gives a pharmacology-free way to separate presynaptic from postsynaptic components.** Mitzdorf proposed using recovery cycles to separate pre- from postsynaptic components ([Mitzdorf 1985](https://doi.org/10.1152/physrev.1985.65.1.37)). The thalamocortical terminal potential ignores the preceding interval while the postsynaptic sink depresses ([Stoelzel et al. 2008](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2713607/)), and V1 adaptation to repeated gratings has intracortical origins ([Westerberg et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6589708/)). If afferent spikes set the MUA's leading edge, the low-threshold lead should survive or grow from presentation 1 to presentations 2 and 3 as the sink weakens. If recurrent build-up sets it, the half-max lead should change with the sink's amplitude instead.

## Conclusion

"Which comes first" has no single answer at the input layer, because neither signal is a single event. Analog MUA mixes presynaptic and postsynaptic spikes, and the sink's amplitude mixes feedforward with recurrent current. The one physiologically constrained quantity, onset to onset, is about 0.7 ms. That is smaller than the 2 ms by which defensible processing choices move our own estimate, and no published pipeline, ours included, has yet resolved it. Our check J is therefore best treated as a calibration, not a discovery about causality. It bounds how far the sink can be trusted as a clock (roughly 0.5–3 ms), and it points to a hypothesis not yet tested on our data: the apparent order depends on whether spikes are measured as analog MUA or as sorted units. The literature's split between "coincident" and "sink first" may come down to that same choice of spike signal. If the unit and repetition checks come out as the afferent account predicts, that observation becomes a small, citable methods result. Otherwise it stays a caution in the supplement.
