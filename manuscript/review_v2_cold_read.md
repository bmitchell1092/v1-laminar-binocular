# Referee report: "The two eyes add first and normalize later in macaque V1"

Paragraph numbers [n] refer to the tags in the submitted text.

---

## 1. What I think the paper claims (after one read)

In awake macaque V1, the way the two eyes' contrast signals combine changes during the first ~50 ms of the response. In the first ~20 ms after (half-maximum) response onset, a binocular grating drives multi-unit activity as if the two eyes' contrasts were summed linearly, with the non-dominant eye weighted by about 0.7. Over the next 20–35 ms the binocular response falls back to the dominant eye's monocular response, and further at higher contrast. The authors attribute this to a delayed interocular normalization pool that weights the non-dominant eye more heavily than the excitatory drive does (w > a). They argue that a latency-robust "equivalent contrast" measure is needed to see this, because binocular responses start earlier than monocular ones. Secondary claims are that the sequence occurs at every depth, that it appears first at the depth of the earliest current sink, and that no fitted model captures the full late collapse, the slow low-contrast rise or the offset response.

I was not sure which of three claims is the main one. It could be the descriptive one (the combination rule changes over time), the methodological one (latency confounds earlier comparisons) or the mechanistic one (a delayed pool with w > a). The abstract gives each roughly equal space, and the status of the laminar claim ("where normalization appeared first") was unclear to me until I reached the paper's own caveat in [51].

## 2. Where I got lost or had to reread

- **[11] Abstract, first paragraph.** It opens with a methodological caveat ("two eyes act as a stronger stimulus than one…") before the reader knows the result. I had to reread it to see why latency matters. "These accounts describe the combination once it has settled" is vague.
- **[12]** "until at 45% contrast the binocular response matched, and in about half of the penetrations fell below, the response of the dominant eye alone": the nested clause breaks the sentence. "Dominant eye" and "penetrations" are used without definition in the abstract.
- **[13]** "the normalization pool weighted the non-dominant eye more than excitation did": more than what? I had to work out that this means relative to the dominant eye, in the pool compared with the drive.
- **[18] → [19].** [18] cites Cox et al. (2019), who show a transient binocular enhancement that is absent in the sustained response. [19] then says "Most of these accounts treat binocular combination as fixed in time." That reads as a contradiction and immediately raises the question of what is new here.
- **[20] → [21].** [20] explains that comparisons "at the same moment" are confounded by latency. [21] then defines the measure as matching "the binocular response at the same moment". The sentence that resolves this is missing: higher monocular contrasts also shorten latency, so matching against the monocular contrast-response function in the same window absorbs the latency advance. Without that sentence the method seems to repeat the error it is meant to fix.
- **[26]** Laminar-alignment procedure in the Results (move it to Methods). "laminar assignments carry an uncertainty of roughly ±0.5–1 mm" stopped me. In a cortex about 2 mm thick, that uncertainty would make every laminar statement in the paper uninterpretable. Is this a typo for ±0.05–0.1 mm?
- **[27]** A one-sentence paragraph. Merge it into Methods, or state what it buys (no interocular timing difference; see concerns).
- **[28]** "the factor that maps the other eye's contrast onto the dominant eye's contrast response": I needed the Methods to parse this. Write it as an equation, R_other(c) ≈ R_dom(a·c). The same paragraph says the dominant eye at 90% is "the level linear summation predicts". That holds only for a = 1; with the stated median a = 0.70 the prediction is 77% (as [33] later says).
- **[25] vs Fig 1a caption and [92].** [25] says "gave each eye its own monitor", while the caption and Methods say each eye viewed one half of a single display. Which is it? For a millisecond-scale binocular paper this matters.
- **[32]** "Response onset" is defined as the time at which the 90% dominant-eye response reaches half its peak. That is a half-maximum latency, not an onset, and the response is visibly rising before t = 0 in Fig 2a. Call it a half-maximum latency, or define it in the abstract.
- **[34]** The linearity index is introduced with "(Methods)". The formula (k − 1)/a is short and should be in the Results. I had to go to [108] to learn what a value of 1.2 means.
- **[35]** Early values of 1.19 and 1.20 are described as "close to 1". They are supralinear, and at 45% the early value is a lower bound because of censoring ([87]; 45% of channels). The reader only learns this in the Limitations.
- **[35] vs [36].** [35] reports p ≤ 0.001 at 11 and 22.5%. [36] says the drop at those contrasts "was weaker and depended on the window choice and subset". I could not tell how robust the effect is outside 45%.
- **[40] vs [43].** [40] says higher contrast made the gain disappear "sooner". [43] says the collapse was "not reliably earlier". Fig 3d visibly shows medians of 35, 30 and 15 ms.
- **[41]** The 20–50 ms window is not one of the windows listed in [108]. Where does it come from?
- **[42]** I reread this three times. Are the "independent recordings" separate runs on the same day, with the same channels and the stimulus moved to the other probe's receptive field? The text also says "five dual-probe days", while [95] says seven.
- **[43]** "Ocularity invariance" is first named here, but the idea was introduced without the name in [17].
- **[47]** "It did not (Fig 4b)." The figure shows a clear inverted-U profile in the late index (about 0.2 superficially and deep, about 0.85 at 1.0–1.8 mm). The text and the figure disagree.
- **[49]** "Normalization was therefore absent from the earliest input at onset, and it appeared in the currents and in the spikes within milliseconds of each other." The "therefore" does not follow from the preceding sentences, which say that the current's binocular gain outlasted the spikes' gain by 10–15 ms.
- **[50]–[51] vs Fig 4d.** The text says each group normalized about 27.5 ms after its own onset, but the reported medians (onset at 30 ms, normalization at 63 ms at the sink) give about 33 ms. The text says "above" responds 4 ms after the sink; the figure shows about 6 ms. The text says "below" normalizes 7 ms after the sink; the figure shows about 12 ms. These may be medians of paired differences versus differences of medians, but the reader cannot tell.
- **[57] vs [28].** The symbol a has a median of 0.88 in the model and 0.70 in the data, and some model values exceed 1 (Fig 5c, a ≈ 1.5), which is impossible for a "non-dominant" eye. Two different quantities share one symbol.
- **[59] vs [113].** The Results say the single-stage model is reached "when the first stage saturates". The Methods say it is reached "as S grows", which makes the first stage linear, not saturating.
- **[60]** Model fits switch from penetration averages to individual channels without comment.
- **[65]** "its rise … [is a prediction] it was not fitted to". The rise (30–80 ms) lies inside the 0–219 ms fit window. Only the offset lies outside it.
- **[75]** A new result (isolated units mostly net-suppressed, MUA mostly facilitated) appears for the first time in the Discussion, cited to "Supplementary".
- **[110]** "registered direction tests" is never explained.
- **Fig 1b caption.** "monocular cells, dioptic cells and dichoptic cells … three cells were shown per fixation trial". Every neurophysiologist will read "cell" as a neuron. I read the caption twice before realizing it means a cell of the 6 × 6 condition grid.

## 5. Scientific concerns, ranked by severity

### Major

**1. What the index measures, and whether the "sequence" requires delayed normalization.**
A linearity index of 1 means the second eye acts like extra contrast in the dominant eye's channel. It does not mean normalization is absent: any normalization model whose drive and pool weight the eyes equally (w = a) gives an index of 1 at every time. The early monocular CRF in Fig 2b is already compressive, so normalization is acting at onset. Moreover, a static normalization model with w > a also predicts an index near 1 during the rising phase (when the pool term is below σ) and a decline afterwards, more so at high contrast, because the pool only exceeds σ once the response is large. The "add first, normalize later" sequence may therefore follow from any normalization model acting on a rising input. The static-versus-delayed comparison ([56], Fig 5b) is based on total held-out error, which the monocular transient-to-sustained shape (the textbook signature of delayed normalization) will dominate. That comparison does not test the binocular claim.
Requests: (a) show the static and delayed models' predicted index time courses against Fig 2c; (b) compare static and delayed models on the interaction loss alone; (c) fit a model with separate monocular and interocular pool delays. Only (c) can support the Discussion heading "Interocular normalization is slower than monocular normalization" ([76]). That heading currently rests on a comparison with anesthetized single units (Albrecht et al. 2002), while the authors' own Fig 6a shows monocular normalization developing over tens of milliseconds, and their own model uses one common pool delay for both eyes. The title should be revised to describe what is measured: the non-dominant eye's net contribution changes from summation-like to suppression-like.

**2. The equivalent-contrast measure may not fully remove latency and waveform-shape effects.**
The method assumes that at every time point the monocular response rises monotonically with contrast, so that the binocular response can be read off as a contrast. During the transient-to-sustained transition, higher contrasts peak earlier and decay faster (Fig 6a). The CRF can then be non-monotonic in a window; the authors acknowledge this implicitly by imposing a running maximum ([107]). A binocular response that is simply an earlier, higher-contrast waveform will also fall earlier, and on that falling flank it can map to a low or even negative index with no change in the rule. The pieces of evidence most exposed to this are the 20–50 ms window used for the contrast-dependence test (Fig 3b–c, "26/26"), the transient negative dip at 45% (Fig 2c, about 45 ms), the biphasic difference waves (Fig 3a) and the MUA undershoot (Fig 4c). Added to this, the early index at 45% is a lower bound in 45% of channels ([87]), and interpolation across the 45–90% interval is coarse.
Requests: (a) a fixed-rule null run through the identical pipeline: build synthetic binocular responses as the monocular response at (1 + a)c at every time point (from a smooth contrast × time fit to the monocular data), add matched noise, and show that the index stays flat; (b) report how often the running maximum and censoring were active in each window; (c) replace or latency-correct the raw "binocular gain" panels (3a, 4c), which use the measure the paper itself calls confounded; (d) show the binocular latency shift directly.

**3. Selection of the dominant eye biases the comparison (regression to the mean).**
The dominant eye is the eye with the larger 90% response over 40–250 ms ([105]). That window overlaps the late analysis window, and the selection is made on the same trials used for analysis. Most MUA channels are near-binocular (Fig 1f, ODI ≈ 0), so for many channels the choice is driven by noise, and the "dominant" responses will be inflated. That inflation lowers the binocular-minus-dominant comparison, especially late and at high contrast, which is the reported pattern (late; 45%; below zero). Fig 1e's divergence, largest at the selection contrast, fits this.
Requests: assign the dominant eye on odd trials and analyse even trials, or use a fixed reference (contralateral eye, or the mean of the two monocular responses); stratify the results by ODI.

**4. MUA is a population sum, and the neuron-level claim is untested.**
The title and abstract are framed as how "the two eyes" combine in V1. A multi-unit signal from neurons with different eye preferences will sum the two eyes' inputs across neurons even if no single neuron sums them. Interocular suppression of monocular neurons (Dougherty et al. 2019) could then produce the late "normalization" in MUA. The authors' own unit result ([75]: units mostly net-suppressed, MUA mostly facilitated), which appears only in the Discussion, suggests that this distinction matters.
Request: show the early and late index for isolated units (about 58), with the caveat of small n, and analyse MUA by ODI bin.

**5. Novelty relative to prior work, including on this dataset.**
The abstract states that the time course "is not known" ([11]), yet Cox et al. (2019, "Temporal dynamics of binocular integration in primary visual cortex") reported early binocular enhancement that is absent in the sustained response. The present dataset has also been analysed before (Mitchell et al. 2023). The paper must state precisely what is new: whether the Cox et al. transient is a latency artefact, the latency-controlled measure, the contrast dependence, the laminar resolution and the dynamic model. It should also explain how the late-window result relates to Mitchell et al. (2023). Relevant work that is not cited and should be discussed or checked includes: monkey V1 binocular contrast-response functions and interocular-phase effects (e.g., Smith et al. 1997, J Neurophysiol); dichoptic masking dynamics in monkey V1 (e.g., Macknik & Martinez-Conde 2004); and the psychophysical contrast-matching literature (e.g., Legge & Rubin 1981; Baker, Meese & Georgeson 2007), of which the "equivalent contrast" is a neural analogue.

**6. Interocular phase disparity and vergence as an alternative to normalization.**
With identical static gratings in a mirror stereoscope, the interocular phase disparity at each receptive field is fixed by the stereoscope alignment and by trial-to-trial vergence. Binocular responses below the monocular response are the classic signature of a non-preferred interocular phase. Disparity selectivity in V1 also develops over tens of milliseconds (e.g., the coarse-to-fine sharpening reported by Menz & Freeman 2003). The late collapse could therefore reflect emerging disparity tuning rather than a normalization pool. The Limitations ([83]) treat disparity as outside the paper's scope, but it is a confound for the main result.
Requests: report whether vergence was measured (the Methods mention only "analog gaze signals"), relate the late index to fixation disparity across trials if possible, and discuss this alternative explicitly.

### Moderate

**7. The laminar and CSD claims go beyond the evidence and are internally inconsistent.**
The title of the Fig 4 section, the abstract ("in the earliest current sink, where normalization appeared first") and the Discussion ([78]) state that normalization starts in the input layer. Against this: (a) Fig 4c shows the current's binocular gain outlasting the spikes' gain by 10–15 ms ([49]); (b) the equivalent-contrast comparisons between current and spikes were "mixed" and none survived correction; (c) the laminar order in [50]–[51] is uncorrected, at 15 ms resolution, with differences of 2–7 ms, and the authors themselves write that "a finer analysis is needed before this sequence can be stated firmly"; (d) since each group normalizes a fixed time after its own onset, "normalization reaches the sink first" adds nothing to "the sink responds first". The ±0.5–1 mm uncertainty in layer assignment ([26], [84]), depth axes reaching 3 mm and the dismissal of the visible depth profile in Fig 4b ([47]) compound the problem.

**8. Model inference.**
(a) The claim that no model captured the full late collapse ([13], [61], the Fig 5 title) misreads an equivalence test: the single-stage model has no bias in Fig 5e, and it fails only because its CIs are wide. (b) Identifiability: w, a, σ and n trade off, so show parameter recovery from simulated data with w = a before interpreting w > a in 24 of 25 penetrations. (c) Model a (0.88, with values above 1) versus empirical a (0.70). (d) The fitted rise is 2.3× too fast at 11% ([66]) inside the fit window, yet 90% of variance is "explained". Report a noise ceiling (odd–even reliability), and check whether the fitted τ2 ≈ 30 ms is compensating for the rise misfit. (e) Fig 5b's outlier is likely an artefact of the fit window (217 ms stimuli). (f) The two-stage model's 1.5% gain is practically negligible and should not appear in the abstract.

**9. Statistics and replication.**
(a) Fourteen of the 26 penetrations come in same-day pairs from seven dual-probe days, so the penetration is not an independent unit for those days; use the session or a mixed model. (b) The "independent recordings" use the same channels, on the same days, in the same monkey. (c) n varies without explanation (26, 25, 24, 23, 22, 21, 15). (d) The claim that the index falls "below zero" (abstract; [43]: stronger than ocularity invariance) rests on 14 of 26 penetrations below zero and a median of −0.12. That is what noise around 0 produces, and it needs a test against 0, or a bound. In the population mean (Fig 2a–b) the late binocular response equals, not undercuts, the dominant eye. (e) The ratio index is heavy-tailed and clipped. Consider a bounded index or a model-based estimate, and report how many values were clipped. (f) Windows vary across analyses (0–20, 20–50, 50–200, 50–150 ms in Fig 5e) with no stated rationale or correction across windows and contrasts. (g) [36] concedes that the 11 and 22.5% effects depend on window and subset; the abstract should then claim robustness only at 45%.

### Minor
- The interocular timing guarantee (both eyes on the same frame and raster lines) should be stated explicitly. Raster position relative to the photodiode gives a constant offset of up to about 8 ms at 120 Hz; it does not affect relative comparisons, but it matters for absolute latencies such as "63 ms".
- An acausal Gaussian smoothing (σ = 3 ms) blurs onset timing by several ms; state its effect on the 0–20 ms window.
- The Methods contain placeholders ("[species, sex…]", "[verify]", "[title, authors: verify]"), an internal repository name ([118]) and lab-notebook details ("curated Kilosort sorts made in 2023, with a spike-time offset corrected" [97]; "the 3.4 ms offset between the 30 kHz and 1 kHz file clocks" [95]). Either explain them or remove them.
- "Units" in [100] means robust standard deviations, which is confusing in a paper that also has single units.
- Gamma correction, receptive-field eccentricities, grating size and spatial frequency, and trial counts after exclusion are not reported.

## 6. Writing

- **Abstract.** It is about 370 words in four paragraphs, well over the J Neurosci (250) and eLife limits. It opens with a caveat rather than a question. It spends a quarter of its length on the latency problem, and then reports a laminar claim the paper itself does not support and a 1.5% model gain. Cut it to one paragraph: question → approach (equivalent contrast) → main result with numbers → model → implication.
- **Title.** "The two eyes add first and normalize later": eyes do not normalize, and, as noted above, normalization is present early. Consider something like "Binocular combination in macaque V1 shifts from summation to interocular suppression within tens of milliseconds".
- **Protocol language that reads like internal notes:** "a separate test that we have specified but not yet run" ([68]); "Testing such a model is the next step; we have not yet done it" ([81]); "registered direction tests" ([110]); "equivalence margin… with 90% confidence" ([61]); "The rule was fixed before its effect on any result was examined" ([100]). Pre-specification is good practice, but either explain it properly in the Methods or remove it. Do not leave fragments of it in the Results.
- **Jargon and undefined terms:** "cell" for a stimulus condition (Fig 1b); "dioptic"; "ocularity invariance" (first used in [43]); "driven channels"; "interaction loss"; "half-transition time"; "cross-recordings"; "fixed-delay fit" (Fig 6 legend only); "eye weight a" for two different quantities.
- **Number-dense passages.** [35] lists six values inline ("from 1.19… to 0.61… from 1.20 to 0.47… from 0.97 to −0.12"); let Fig 2d carry them and give one or two in the text. [50]–[51] give eight timing numbers with mixed reference points (stimulus clock versus own onset).
- **Non sequiturs:** "Normalization was therefore absent from the earliest input at onset…" ([49]); "It did not (Fig 4b)." ([47]).
- **Curt or orphaned sentences:** [27] (a one-sentence paragraph); "Two cautions follow." ([68]), where the second caution is not a caution.
- **What to keep:** the Introduction's short, declarative style ([16]–[20]) is effective. The explanation of equivalent contrast in [33] is clear once the reader has Fig 2b. The control list in [36] is the right content in the right place.

## 7. My three most important recommendations

1. **Show that the measure is valid before interpreting it.** Run a fixed-rule null through the identical pipeline (synthetic binocular responses equal to the monocular response at (1 + a)c at every time point) and show that the index stays flat, particularly in the 20–50 ms window. Assign the dominant eye with cross-validation (select on odd trials, analyse even). Report how often the running maximum and censoring were active. Show the binocular latency shift directly. Drop the raw-difference panels (3a, 4c) or correct them for latency. Show the result in isolated units, or by ODI bin, so that it is not an artefact of MUA pooling eye-specific neurons.

2. **Reframe the central claim and test it with the model.** An index of 1 means the second eye acts as extra contrast; it does not mean normalization is absent. A static normalization model with w > a may already produce the sequence. Overlay the static and delayed models' predicted index time courses on Fig 2c. Test separate monocular and interocular pool delays before claiming that interocular normalization is slower than monocular normalization. Correct the reading of Fig 5e: the single-stage model is unbiased but imprecise, which is not a failure to capture the collapse. Address phase disparity and vergence as an alternative explanation of the late suppression. Revise the title.

3. **Tighten the scope and the story.** State plainly what is new relative to Cox et al. (2019) and the earlier analysis of this dataset (Mitchell et al. 2023). Remove "normalization appeared first in the earliest sink" from the title, abstract and Discussion unless a finer analysis supports it. Move Fig 6, 3d, 4d and 5d (and the two-stage and Ding–Sperling detail) to the supplement. Add the analysis the 6 × 6 design was built for: iso-response contours in the dominant-eye × other-eye contrast plane, early versus late. That figure would connect directly to the psychophysics and fMRI literature and would likely become the paper's centrepiece. Cut the abstract to about 250 words, remove protocol language, and end with a conclusion and a testable prediction rather than a limitations list.
